# Domain 6 — Fundamental Analysis
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Fundamental analysis evaluates the underlying quality and durability of a business — beyond the numbers — to judge whether it deserves long-term capital allocation.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Business Quality
- Definition: The inherent strength, predictability, and resilience of the business model.
- Assessment factors: Revenue predictability, customer concentration/diversification, pricing power, asset intensity, cyclicality.
- Research use: A high-quality business converts revenue growth into profit growth consistently across cycles.

### 2. Management
- Assessment factors: Track record of execution, capital allocation decisions, communication transparency, promoter integrity, succession planning.
- Research use: Compare stated guidance in past disclosures against actual delivered results to judge credibility.

### 3. Competitive Position
- Frameworks: Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, threat of new entrants); market share trends.
- Research use: Assess whether competitive position is strengthening, stable, or eroding over time using market share and margin trend data.

### 4. Financial Strength
- Assessment factors: Leverage levels, liquidity position, interest coverage, contingent liabilities.
- Research use: A financially strong company can withstand downturns and fund growth without excessive dilution or distress borrowing.

### 5. Cash Generation
- Assessment factors: Operating cash flow consistency, free cash flow conversion, working capital discipline.
- Research use: Sustainable cash generation is a stronger quality signal than reported accounting profit alone.

### 6. Growth
- Assessment factors: Organic vs. inorganic growth, volume vs. price-led growth, addressable market size, reinvestment runway.
- Research use: Distinguish structural growth (secular demand shift) from cyclical growth (temporary demand spike).

### 7. Capital Allocation
- Assessment factors: History of capex decisions, M&A track record, dividend policy, buyback history, return on incremental capital employed.
- Research use: Evaluate whether management has historically deployed capital at returns exceeding cost of capital.

### 8. Moat (Sustainable Competitive Advantage)
- Types: Brand, network effects, switching costs, cost advantage, regulatory/licensing barriers, intangible assets (patents).
- Research use: Assess moat durability against emerging competitive and technological threats — moats can erode over time.

### 9. Governance
- Assessment factors: Board independence, related party transaction disclosure, auditor quality/tenure, promoter pledging, minority shareholder treatment.
- Research use: Governance issues are often leading indicators of future financial or reputational problems (see Domain 8 for detailed framework).

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Build a business quality scorecard across all nine factors above before forming an investment view.
2. Weight factors based on sector context (e.g., moat and pricing power matter more in consumer; capital allocation matters more in cyclicals).
3. Corroborate qualitative assessment with quantitative evidence from financial statements (Domains 2–4).
4. Revisit assessment periodically — business quality is not static.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Overreliance on narrative/story without financial corroboration.
- Assuming a moat is permanent without monitoring for disruption risk.
- Ignoring capital allocation history when projecting future growth via reinvestment.
- Treating management commentary at face value without cross-checking delivery history.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Fundamental analysis conclusions should always be traceable to specific evidence (financial data, disclosures, verifiable track record) — never presented as an unsupported qualitative judgment.

---

## Worked Example
A branded FMCG company raised prices 8% over two years while volume growth held steady at 5-6% — evidence of genuine pricing power (moat intact). A commodity chemical maker raised prices 15% in the same period but volumes fell 12%, with the gain fully explained by industry-wide input cost pass-through, not company-specific advantage. Same "price growth" headline, opposite quality signal.
