# Domain 24 — Financial Quality & Forensic Accounting
Version: v_0.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Detect earnings manipulation, aggressive accounting, and fraud risk before capital is committed — critical for micro/small-cap investing where disclosure quality is inconsistent and promoter integrity varies widely. This is the single highest-leverage screen for avoiding permanent capital loss in small-cap investing.

## Core Principle
Profits can be manipulated. Cash is much harder to fake. Assume every reported number is a claim to be verified, not a fact to be accepted.

## Quantitative Forensic Models

**1. Beneish M-Score** — Statistical model estimating probability of earnings manipulation using 8 variables (DSRI, GMI, AQI, SGI, DEPI, SGAI, LVGI, TATA). Score > -1.78 signals elevated manipulation risk.

**2. Piotroski F-Score** — 9-point financial strength score (profitability, leverage/liquidity, operating efficiency). Score ≥7 = strong; ≤3 = weak. Widely used as a small-cap quality filter.

**3. Altman Z-Score** — Bankruptcy/distress prediction combining working capital, retained earnings, EBIT, market value of equity, and sales, each scaled by total assets. Z < 1.8 = distress zone; Z > 3.0 = safe zone.

**4. Sloan Accrual Ratio** — (Net Income – Operating Cash Flow) / Total Assets. High accruals relative to cash flow predict future earnings disappointment — a core small-cap quality filter.

## Core Red Flag Categories

**1. Earnings Quality**
- Cash Conversion Ratio = CFO / PAT, sustained below 0.7-0.8 is a warning.
- Frequent "exceptional items" normalizing weak core operations.
- Revenue growth without matching receivables discipline (DSO rising faster than sales).

**2. Balance Sheet Red Flags**
- Rapid, unexplained growth in "Other Assets," "Loans & Advances," or intercorporate deposits.
- Goodwill/intangibles ballooning post-acquisition without impairment testing.
- Large contingent liabilities disclosed in fine print but not discussed in MD&A.

**3. Promoter & Governance Forensics**
- Rising promoter pledge % — the single most dangerous small-cap signal; pledged shares can trigger forced selling in a downturn.
- Frequent preferential allotments to promoters at low valuation.
- Auditor resignation mid-year, or repeated small/unknown audit firm changes.
- Complex web of unlisted related entities receiving loans/guarantees from the listed company.

**4. Revenue Red Flags**
- Revenue growth concentrated in Q4 (channel stuffing pattern) not seen in other quarters.
- Large related-party sales inflating topline.
- Sudden unexplained margin expansion without cost/pricing rationale.

## Application Framework (Small/Micro-Cap Specific)
1. **Pledge check first** — any promoter pledge >25% is an automatic caution flag for swing/positional entries.
2. **CFO/PAT trend over 3 years**, not one year — one bad year can be explained; three years of divergence cannot.
3. **Cross-check auditor tenure and size** — Big-4/reputed regional auditor vs. unknown small firm is a meaningful signal in micro-caps.
4. **Screen for related-party revenue %** disclosed in notes — anything above 10-15% of revenue needs scrutiny.
5. Run Piotroski F-Score as a fast pre-screen before deeper qualitative work — cheap to compute, high signal for eliminating weak candidates from a large watchlist.

## Worked Example
A micro-cap shows PAT growth of 45% YoY, but CFO actually declined. Notes reveal trade receivables grew 60% and a new related entity (promoter-linked) accounts for 20% of incremental revenue. Auditor changed twice in 18 months. Individually explainable; together this is a textbook manipulation cluster — pass regardless of how attractive the chart looks.

## Red Flags Checklist (Quick Screen)
- Promoter pledge rising or >25%
- CFO/PAT < 0.7 for 2+ consecutive years
- Auditor changed in last 12 months without clear reason
- Related-party revenue/loans >10% of base
- Beneish M-Score > -1.78
- Piotroski F-Score ≤ 3
- Frequent preferential allotments at discount to market

## AI Trigger Keywords
is this fraud, forensic, manipulation, red flag, promoter pledge, auditor change, M-Score, F-Score, Z-Score, fake profit, accounting quality, is this safe to buy.

## Universal Rule Applied
Never conclude fraud from a single indicator. Require a cluster of 2+ independent red flags before assigning high manipulation risk, and always disclose which specific indicators triggered the flag.

## Forensic Gate — Conflict Resolution Rules

**Rule 1 — Forensic flags override all positive signals:**  
A forensic FAIL verdict overrides a positive technical chart pattern, cheap valuation, strong sector tailwind, or institutional buying — without exception. This is codified in `00_Index.md` Global Conflict Arbitration Rule #1: "Forensic red flags (24) and Governance red flags (8) override everything."

**Rule 2 — A single flag requires scrutiny, not automatic exit:**  
One isolated flag (e.g., CFO/PAT slightly below 0.7 for one year during a high-growth capex cycle) is a watch item, not a hard exit. Require a second independent flag from a different category before raising to CAUTION or FAIL. Never conclude fraud from a single indicator.

**Rule 3 — Positive stock price action does not reduce flag severity:**  
A rising stock price, recent mutual fund buying, or analyst upgrades do NOT reduce the risk associated with active forensic flags. Manipulation often occurs precisely when stock narratives are strongest. If the gate flags are present, they must be resolved with evidence (e.g., auditor explanation, CFO/PAT recovery for 2+ years) before the flag is cleared — not dismissed because price is rising.

# Domain 25 — Moat & Competitive Advantage
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain, elevated from Domain 6)

## Purpose
Two companies with identical revenue, profit, and ROCE can produce dramatically different long-term returns — the difference is almost always moat durability. This domain must be assessed before any valuation work, since valuation multiples are meaningless without first knowing how long a company can defend its returns.

## Core Concept
An economic moat is a structural competitive advantage enabling returns on capital above cost of capital for a sustained period, despite competitive pressure.

## Sources of Moat (India-Specific Examples)

| Source | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Network Effects | Value rises with each added user | UPI ecosystem, NSE, exchanges |
| Switching Costs | High friction to change suppliers | Enterprise IT, ERP/Tally, loyalty-driven retail |
| Cost Advantage | Structural lower cost than peers | Large-scale manufacturers, integrated players |
| Intangible Assets | Brand, patents, licenses, approvals | Consumer brands, pharma ANDA portfolios |
| Efficient Scale | Market too small for a 2nd profitable entrant | Niche infra/utility monopolies |
| Data Advantage | Proprietary data improves decisions | Credit bureaus, insurance underwriting data |
| Distribution Advantage | Superior reach/access to customers | Deep dealer/distributor networks |
| Regulatory Barriers | Licenses, natural monopoly protection | Airports, exchanges, regulated utilities |
| Ecosystem Lock-in | Interlocking products create switching cost | Financial services groups, platform bundles |

## Moat Durability Classification

| Rating | Characteristics | Implication for Entry |
|---|---|---|
| Wide Moat | Durable 15-20+ years, multiple sources, proven across cycles | Premium valuation justified; core long-term holding candidate |
| Narrow Moat | Durable 5-15 years, single source, moderate | Moderate premium; monitor for erosion |
| No Moat | Advantage absent or purely temporary/cyclical | Only attractive if deeply undervalued, time-limited thesis |
| Eroding Moat | Once existed, now under structural pressure | Extreme caution; avoid unless deep-value/turnaround thesis with clear catalyst |

## Widening vs. Eroding Signals

**Widening:**
- Market share rising without price wars
- Returns on capital improving over time, not just revenue
- Customer retention/repeat-purchase rates improving
- New product/category extensions succeeding
- Weaker competitors exiting or losing share

**Eroding:**
- Pricing power declining (discounts/promotions increasing to hold volume)
- Slow, steady market share loss
- New technology or business model threatening obsolescence
- Low-cost disruptors gaining traction
- Regulatory protection being removed or diluted
- Management diversifying away from the core moat business (capital misallocation signal)

## Disruption Risk Checklist
1. Technology disruption — could AI/automation/platform shift make the model obsolete?
2. Business-model disruption — could D2C/platform/subscription models bypass this business?
3. Regulatory disruption — could deregulation remove the protective barrier?
4. Global competition — could international entrants erode local advantage?
5. Demographic shift — is the target customer base growing, flat, or shrinking?

## Application Framework — Small/Mid-Cap Specific
For emerging small/mid-caps, moat is often **not yet fully built** — the real question is: *is the moat being constructed, and is there evidence of it starting to work?* Look for: rising market share in a niche, improving pricing power in a specific micro-segment, early signs of switching costs forming (contract renewals, repeat orders), or a regulatory/licensing edge not yet priced in. This is different from mature large-cap moat assessment — you're underwriting moat formation, not moat maturity.

## Worked Example
A niche auto-ancillary company supplies a critical component to 3 major OEMs under multi-year qualification contracts (2-3 year re-qualification cycle for any new supplier). Its market share in this specific component category rose from 12% to 19% over 4 years while maintaining stable EBITDA margins even as steel costs fluctuated — evidence of both a switching-cost moat (OEM qualification) and pricing power. This is a narrow-but-widening moat, appropriate for a positional/SIP-style accumulation thesis, distinct from a pure momentum trade.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Assuming a moat is permanent without monitoring disruption risk each cycle.
- Confusing temporary pricing power (commodity up-cycle) with a genuine structural moat.
- Over-crediting "brand" moat without evidence of actual pricing power or retention data.
- In small-caps, mistaking a single large customer/contract concentration for a moat — that's concentration risk, not durability.

## AI Trigger Keywords
moat, competitive advantage, pricing power, market share trend, is this a good business, switching cost, brand strength, durable advantage.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 5 (moat durability directly justifies valuation premium) · Domain 6 (fundamental quality corroboration) · Domain 12 (industry structure context) · Domain 26 (M&A rationale often moat-related).

## Conflict Rule
Moat assessment governs valuation premium/discount decisions (Domain 5) — never assign a premium multiple to a "no moat" or "eroding moat" business regardless of near-term growth or momentum signals.

## Universal Rule Applied
Never assign a positive long-term thesis based on moat alone — moat must be corroborated with financial evidence (Domain 4 returns trend, Domain 2 cash generation) before concluding durability.

# Domain 26 — Financial Institution Analysis
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Banks, NBFCs, insurers, and AMCs require a fundamentally different analytical framework than industrial companies — standard P&L/balance sheet analysis (Domain 2-5) misapplies here. This domain provides the correct statement structure, metrics, and valuation logic for financial institutions.

## Why Standard Frameworks Fail Here
- Revenue/COGS structure doesn't exist — income is interest/premium/fee-based.
- "Debt" is the raw material (deposits/borrowings), not a leverage red flag by default.
- Working capital concepts (Domain 3) don't apply.
- P/E is less useful than P/B or embedded value for parts of this sector.

## A. Banking

**Key Statement Items:** Net Interest Income (NII), Non-Interest/Fee Income, Operating Expenses, Pre-Provision Operating Profit (PPOP), Provisions, PAT.

**Core Metrics:**
- Net Interest Margin (NIM) = NII / Average Interest-Earning Assets
- Gross NPA % and Net NPA % — asset quality
- Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR) — cushion against bad loans
- Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) — regulatory solvency buffer
- CASA Ratio — low-cost deposit mix (higher = cheaper funding = structural advantage)
- Credit-to-Deposit Ratio — growth capacity signal
- Cost-to-Income Ratio — operating efficiency
- Return on Assets (ROA), Return on Equity (ROE)
- Slippage Ratio — fresh NPA formation rate (forward-looking asset quality signal)

**Valuation:** P/B (Price-to-Book) is primary, adjusted for ROE — higher sustainable ROE justifies higher P/B. P/E used secondarily. Compare P/B against sustainable ROE, not book value alone.

## B. NBFC (Non-Banking Financial Companies)

**Core Metrics:**
- AUM (Assets Under Management) growth
- NIM, Cost of Funds, spread over borrowing cost
- GNPA/NNPA — asset quality (often lags banks in disclosure rigor — scrutinize harder)
- Leverage (Debt/Equity) — NBFCs typically run higher leverage; assess against liability mix diversification
- Liability mix — diversified (bonds, CP, bank lines) vs. concentrated funding is a key risk differentiator
- ALM (Asset-Liability Mismatch) — critical vulnerability; short-term borrowing funding long-term assets is a red flag (2018 IL&FS-style risk)

**Valuation:** P/B adjusted for ROE, similar to banks; also P/E where earnings are stable.

## C. Insurance

**Core Metrics:**
- New Business Premium (NBP) — new sales momentum
- Value of New Business (VNB) Margin — profitability of new policies sold
- Persistency Ratio — policy renewal/retention rate (13th month, 61st month persistency commonly tracked)
- Embedded Value (EV) — PV of future profits from existing policies + net worth
- Combined Ratio (General Insurance) = (Claims + Expenses) / Premium; <100% = underwriting profit
- Solvency Ratio — regulatory capital adequacy (IRDAI mandated minimum)

**Valuation:** P/EV (Price to Embedded Value) is the primary metric for life insurers — not P/E or P/B.

## D. AMC (Asset Management Companies)

**Core Metrics:**
- AUM growth and mix (equity vs. debt — equity AUM carries higher margins)
- Yield/Revenue as % of AUM
- SIP book size and growth (recurring, sticky revenue base)
- Market share trend in active/passive segments
- Operating leverage — AMCs scale profit faster than AUM growth due to largely fixed cost base

**Valuation:** P/E on normalized earnings; also EV/AUM for relative comparison.

## Application Framework
1. Identify sub-category (bank/NBFC/insurer/AMC) before applying any metric — do not use bank NIM logic on an AMC.
2. Prioritize asset quality trend (NPA/slippage) over headline profit growth — provisioning cycles can mask true credit quality for several quarters.
3. For NBFCs, weight liability-side analysis (ALM, funding diversity) as heavily as asset-side growth — funding risk is the dominant failure mode in this sector.
4. Use P/B-vs-ROE framework for banks/NBFCs, P/EV for life insurers, EV/AUM or P/E for AMCs — never force a single valuation lens across all four sub-sectors.

## Worked Example
An NBFC shows AUM growth of 35% YoY (headline looks excellent) but funding is 70% short-term commercial paper against a loan book with average tenure of 3 years — a clear asset-liability mismatch. If CP rollover becomes difficult in a tight liquidity environment (as in 2018), this NBFC faces a funding crisis despite a "growing" loan book. Growth alone is not the signal; funding structure is.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Rising GNPA with declining Provision Coverage Ratio simultaneously (double negative — deteriorating quality with shrinking cushion).
- NBFC funding concentrated in short-term instruments against long-tenure assets.
- Insurance persistency ratio declining while NBP is rising (indicates aggressive new sales masking poor retention/quality).
- Banks/NBFCs showing NIM expansion purely from delayed provisioning rather than genuine margin improvement.

## AI Trigger Keywords
bank stock, NBFC, NIM, NPA, CASA, insurance stock, embedded value, AMC, AUM, combined ratio, persistency, provision coverage, capital adequacy.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 2 (statement structure differs — use this domain's version) · Domain 4 (standard ratios largely don't apply here) · Domain 20 (credit market signals highly relevant for NBFC funding risk) · Domain 9 (Liquidity Risk is dominant for NBFCs).

## Conflict Rule
For financial institutions, this domain's metrics override generic Domain 4 ratio benchmarks entirely — P/E, current ratio, and standard leverage ratios from Domain 4 should not be applied to banks/NBFCs/insurers.

## Universal Rule Applied
Financial institution disclosures (asset quality, provisioning) are judgment-heavy areas prone to smoothing — always cross-check management-reported NPA figures against RBI/rating agency data where available rather than accepting company-disclosed numbers at face value.

# Domain 27 — Super-Investor Tracking & Smart Money Signals
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Ace-investor style research (Vijay Kedia, Ashish Kacholia, Mukul Agarwal, Dolly Khanna-style approach) is built on systematically tracking disclosed institutional/individual ownership changes in small and micro-caps — treating public shareholding data as an early signal generator, not a standalone buy trigger.

## Core Principle
Smart money entry is a **screening signal**, not a reason by itself. These investors are right often enough to be worth tracking, but they also enter early and are sometimes wrong — always validate with independent fundamental work (Domains 4, 6, 24) before acting.

## Core Data Sources & Signals

**1. Shareholding Pattern Disclosure (>1% Public Shareholders)**
- Companies disclose all shareholders holding >1% of equity in quarterly shareholding pattern filings.
- Track quarter-on-quarter changes: new entries, increased stakes, reduced/exited stakes among known ace investors.
- A fresh entry by a tracked investor into a sub-₹500cr market cap company is a high-value signal worth deeper research.

**2. Bulk Deals & Block Deals**
- Bulk deal: >0.5% of company's shares traded by single entity in a day, disclosed on exchange.
- Block deal: large pre-negotiated trade in a separate window, disclosed separately.
- Track: who is buying (known smart money vs. unknown entity), price paid relative to market price, and whether it's an accumulation pattern (multiple bulk deals over weeks) vs. single event.

**3. Insider/Promoter Buying**
- Promoter or designated-person buying (disclosed under PIT regulations) in the open market is a strong conviction signal — especially meaningful when it happens after a price decline, not during a rally.
- Distinguish open-market buying (genuine conviction) from ESOP allotment or preferential issue (not the same signal).

**4. Mutual Fund / FII Entry into Small-Caps**
- New mutual fund scheme entries into a small-cap (visible in monthly portfolio disclosures) often precede broader re-rating as more funds follow.
- FII entry into a micro-cap is rarer and often signals a structural re-rating thesis forming.

## Application Framework — How to Use This Signal
1. **Screen first**: Set up tracking for a watchlist of 15-25 known smart-money investors' quarterly shareholding changes.
2. **Filter for fresh/increasing positions** in companies below your market-cap comfort zone (micro/small-cap).
3. **Cross-validate immediately** with Domain 24 (Forensic Accounting) — smart money is not immune to buying into companies that later show red flags; never skip the forensic screen just because a known investor is in.
4. **Check entry price vs. current price** — if the stock has already run up significantly since the disclosed entry, the "easy" part of the move may be over; assess whether the original thesis still holds at current valuation.
5. **Look for clustering** — multiple tracked investors independently entering the same stock within 1-2 quarters is a stronger signal than a single investor's position.

## Worked Example
A tracked investor's shareholding filing shows a fresh 2.1% stake in a ₹350cr market cap specialty chemicals company, entered in the last quarter at an estimated average price of ₹180 (back-calculated from disclosed value/date range). Stock now trades at ₹210. Cross-check: Piotroski F-Score = 7, no promoter pledge, CFO/PAT ratio healthy at 0.85. This combination — smart money entry + clean forensic screen + reasonable premium to entry price — is a valid candidate for deeper fundamental research, not yet a buy signal on its own.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Chasing a stock purely because a well-known name appears in shareholding data, without independent verification — many trades are exits in progress or portfolio rebalancing, not fresh high-conviction bets.
- Late discovery: by the time shareholding data is publicly disclosed (quarterly lag), a meaningful part of any re-rating may have already occurred.
- Survivorship bias: publicly celebrated ace-investor picks are the winners; their less-publicized misses in the same portfolios are less visible — don't assume a 100% hit rate.
- Operator-style "smart money" (undisclosed entities orchestrating price moves) can mimic genuine accumulation patterns — always pair with forensic and governance screens (Domain 24, Domain 8).

Kedia stock, Kacholiya stock, Mukul Agarwal portfolio, ace investor, smart money, bulk deal, block deal, shareholding pattern, who is buying this stock, insider buying, promoter buying.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 24 (mandatory forensic cross-check before acting) · Domain 8 (governance quality of the same company) · Domain 28 (multibagger identification overlap) · Domain 14 (bulk/block deal mechanics).

## Conflict Rule
A smart-money entry signal never overrides a forensic red flag (Domain 24) or governance red flag (Domain 8) — this domain generates candidates for research, it does not clear them for entry.

## Universal Rule Applied
Never present a smart-money signal as a standalone recommendation — always disclose that shareholding data has a reporting lag and that the original investor's entry price/rationale is not fully known, only inferable.

# Domain 28 — Multibagger & Turnaround Identification Framework
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Systematic framework for identifying small/micro-cap companies with genuine potential for multi-year, multi-fold returns — distinguishing structural multibagger candidates from stocks that merely look cheap or are rallying on momentum without a durable driver.

## Core Multibagger Pattern (The Compounding Chain)
Small base → Catalyst emerges → Earnings inflect → Market re-rates (P/E expansion) → Scalability sustains growth → Repeat.
A true multibagger typically delivers returns from **both** earnings growth AND multiple re-rating simultaneously — this combination is what produces 5-10x+ returns, not either alone.

## Category A: Early Multibagger Screen

**1. Small Revenue/Profit Base with Large Addressable Market**
- Company revenue is a tiny fraction of total addressable market (TAM) — headroom for years of compounding growth.
- Prefer businesses where even modest market share gains translate to large percentage revenue growth off a small base.

**2. Catalyst Identification**
- New capacity coming online (check capex disclosure, expected commissioning date).
- New product/client win disclosed in exchange filing or investor presentation.
- Regulatory tailwind specific to this company (PLI eligibility, import substitution opportunity, new licensing).
- Management/promoter change bringing fresh execution capability.

**3. Scalability Evidence**
- Operating leverage: fixed cost base means incremental revenue drops disproportionately to profit.
- Working capital efficiency improving as scale increases (not deteriorating).
- Evidence of successful capacity utilization ramp-up in a prior expansion cycle (track record).

**4. Early Re-Rating Signals**
- Institutional/smart money entry (cross-reference Domain 27).
- Analyst coverage initiation (first broker reports appearing) — often precedes broader market awareness.
- Peer companies in the same theme already re-rated, and this company is a laggard with similar fundamentals.

## Category B: Turnaround Screen

**1. Root-Cause Resolution (not symptom management)**
- Identify what specifically broke the business (debt overhang, one bad segment, management issue, industry downturn) and verify it's genuinely being fixed, not just described as being fixed.
- New management with a credible track record of fixing similar situations elsewhere.

**2. Balance Sheet Repair Evidence**
- Debt reduction trend over multiple quarters (not just one-time asset sale masking ongoing operational weakness).
- Working capital cycle normalizing.
- Interest coverage ratio improving sequentially.

**3. Operational Inflection Signals**
- Sequential (QoQ) margin improvement, not just YoY (YoY can be a low-base illusion).
- Order book/enquiry levels recovering ahead of revenue recovery (leading indicator).
- Capacity utilization bottoming and turning up.

**4. Distinguishing Genuine Turnaround from Value Trap**
- Genuine: structural fix in place + industry backdrop stable/improving + management credibility verified.
- Value trap: cheap valuation persists because the core problem (competitive position, industry structural decline, governance) hasn't actually changed — only optics improved (e.g., one-time gain flattering a quarter).

## Category C: SIP-Growth / Compounding Candidate Screen
(Different from early multibagger — steadier, lower-volatility compounding for positional/SIP-style accumulation)
- Consistent 15-25% revenue/PAT CAGR over 5+ years with low earnings volatility.
- ROE/ROCE consistently >15-18% without excessive leverage.
- Reinvestment runway still long (low dividend payout, high retained earnings redeployed at good returns).
- Management with a demonstrated multi-year capital allocation discipline (not a single good year).

## Category D: Volume Story / Uptrend Screen
(Technical corroboration layer — cross-reference Domain 30 for full technical framework)
- Unusual volume spike (2-3x+ average) accompanying a price move, especially breaking out of a multi-month base.
- Delivery percentage (not just traded volume) rising — signals genuine accumulation vs. pure intraday speculation.
- Price sustaining above breakout level for several sessions rather than a single-day spike that fades.

## Application Framework — Combined Screen
1. Run Category A or B screen to generate a fundamental candidate.
2. Cross-validate with Domain 24 (Forensic Accounting) — mandatory gate.
3. Check Domain 27 (Super-Investor Tracking) for corroborating smart-money entry.
4. Layer Category D (volume/technical) for entry timing only — never as the primary thesis driver.
5. Size position per Domain 29 (Micro-Cap Risk) given the inherently higher risk/volatility of this category.

## Worked Example
A specialty engineering company has revenue of ₹120cr against an estimated TAM of ₹8,000cr (import-substitution opportunity from a recent government policy). New capacity doubling output comes online next quarter. Operating margin improved from 8% to 12% over the last 4 quarters purely from utilization gains on existing assets (before new capacity even adds volume) — clear operating leverage evidence. A tracked ace investor took a fresh 1.8% stake last quarter. Forensic screen is clean (no pledge, healthy CFO/PAT). This is a well-formed early multibagger candidate — the combination of small base, real catalyst (capacity + policy), proven operating leverage, and smart money corroboration is what separates it from a story stock.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Confusing narrative ("this could be the next X") with verified catalyst evidence (actual capex/order/policy disclosure).
- Buying purely on a "re-rating" thesis without underlying earnings growth to sustain it (multiple expansion alone reverses quickly).
- Ignoring the forensic screen because the growth story is exciting — small-cap fraud clusters disproportionately in "story stocks."
- Averaging down repeatedly on a "turnaround" thesis without re-verifying that the root cause is actually being fixed each quarter.

## AI Trigger Keywords
multibagger, turnaround stock, next multibagger, early stage stock, growth stock, compounding stock, SIP growth stock, is this a good long-term stock, capacity expansion story, import substitution.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 24 (mandatory forensic gate) · Domain 27 (smart money corroboration) · Domain 16 (Special Situations — turnaround formal framework) · Domain 30 (technical entry timing) · Domain 29 (position sizing given risk).

## Conflict Rule
A compelling multibagger narrative never overrides a forensic red flag or a genuinely unresolved turnaround root cause — narrative excitement is explicitly flagged in Domain 11 (Behavioural Finance) as a bias to guard against here.

## Universal Rule Applied
Distinguish disclosed, verifiable catalysts (capex filings, order wins, policy documents) from inferred or narrative-based catalysts — always cite the specific disclosure source for any catalyst claim.

# Domain 29 — Micro/Small-Cap Risk Framework
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Micro and small-cap investing carries structurally different risks than large-cap investing — liquidity, disclosure quality, and manipulation risk are materially higher. This domain provides risk controls specific to this market-cap segment, essential given the swing/positional trading style this library is built for.

## Core Principle
The same fundamental thesis that would be "safe enough" in a large-cap requires additional risk controls in a micro-cap purely due to structural factors — position sizing and exit discipline matter as much as stock selection here.

## Core Risk Categories

**1. Liquidity Risk**
- Average daily trading volume/value determines your realistic position size and exit speed.
- Rule of thumb: avoid taking a position that would represent more than 10-15% of a stock's average daily traded value — larger positions risk moving the price against you on exit.
- Illiquid stocks can gap down significantly on bad news with no buyers at intermediate prices — exit risk is asymmetric to entry risk.

**2. Price Manipulation / Operator Risk**
- Circular trading patterns: repeated buying/selling among a small group of connected entities to create artificial volume/price trends.
- Sudden unexplained price run-ups with no corresponding fundamental news or disclosure — check exchange surveillance actions (GSM/ASM categorization by SEBI/exchanges).
- Watch for: stocks moved to Graded Surveillance Measure (GSM) or Additional Surveillance Measure (ASM) lists — these carry trading restrictions and elevated risk.

**3. Disclosure Quality Risk**
- Small-caps often have weaker investor relations, less analyst coverage, and lower disclosure rigor than large-caps — information asymmetry is higher.
- Annual report quality varies widely; some micro-caps provide minimal MD&A detail compared to institutional-grade disclosure.
- Verify basic facts (registered office, auditor identity, related-party details) independently rather than assuming standard-quality disclosure.

**4. Promoter Concentration Risk**
- In micro-caps, business outcomes are often disproportionately dependent on a single promoter/founder's decisions and integrity — succession risk and key-person risk are elevated.
- Promoter pledge (cross-reference Domain 24) is a sharper risk signal here than in large-caps, since forced selling on pledge invocation can be a larger % of free float.

**5. Circuit Filter Risk**
- Many small/micro-caps trade with tighter daily circuit filters (2%/5%/10%) — this limits your ability to exit quickly in a falling market; a stock can be "locked" at the lower circuit with no buyers for multiple sessions.
- Factor this into position sizing: assume worst-case exit may take several sessions, not one.

**6. Corporate Action / Dilution Risk**
- Small-caps more frequently use preferential allotments, warrants, and rights issues that can dilute existing shareholders — verify whether recent/planned issuances are at fair value or favor insiders.

## Application Framework — Position Sizing & Risk Controls
1. **Cap individual micro/small-cap position size** meaningfully smaller than a large-cap position in the same portfolio — reflect the elevated single-stock risk in sizing, not just conviction.
2. **Check GSM/ASM status** before entry — avoid or heavily discount conviction on stocks under active surveillance restriction.
3. **Verify average daily traded value** covers your intended position size comfortably on both entry and likely exit.
4. **Set a hard stop-loss level before entry**, sized wider than a large-cap stop to account for circuit-filter gap risk, but still bounded — never average down into an illiquid, deteriorating position.
5. **Always run Domain 24 (Forensic) and Domain 8 (Governance) screens** — these matter more here than in any other segment.
6. **Diversify across positions** rather than concentrating — single-stock idiosyncratic risk (fraud, promoter issue, sudden delisting risk) is higher in this segment than sector or macro risk.

## Worked Example
A micro-cap trades ₹15 lakh average daily value. A trader considers a ₹3 lakh position (20% of daily value) — this exceeds the 10-15% guideline and risks meaningful slippage on both entry and exit, plus the stock has a 5% circuit filter. Sizing down to ₹1.5-2 lakh keeps the position within a realistic liquidity comfort zone, and the trader pre-defines a stop-loss level accounting for the possibility of a locked lower circuit for 1-2 sessions before an exit is achievable.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Position sizing based purely on conviction/story attractiveness without a liquidity check.
- Ignoring GSM/ASM surveillance status because "the fundamentals look fine."
- Treating a micro-cap stop-loss the same way as a liquid large-cap stop — execution risk differs materially.
- Concentrating a large % of portfolio in a single micro-cap "high conviction" idea without diversification discipline.

## AI Trigger Keywords
micro cap risk, small cap risk, liquidity risk, GSM, ASM, circuit filter, is this stock safe, position size, how much to buy, penny stock risk, operator stock.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 24 (forensic screen — mandatory pairing) · Domain 8 (governance/promoter risk) · Domain 10 (portfolio-level position sizing rules) · Domain 14 (circuit filter/surveillance mechanics).

## Conflict Rule
Liquidity and surveillance-status risk override attractiveness of the fundamental/technical thesis when sizing a position — a great story in a GSM-flagged, illiquid stock still gets a small, risk-adjusted position, never a large one.

## Universal Rule Applied
Always disclose the liquidity profile (average daily traded value, circuit filter band, surveillance status) alongside any micro/small-cap recommendation — this context is as important as the fundamental thesis itself for this market-cap segment.

# Domain 30 — Swing & Positional Technical Patterns
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Domain 7 covers general technical analysis; this domain provides the specific pattern vocabulary and timeframe discipline for 2-day to 10-day swing trades and 1-week to 2-month positional trades — the holding periods this library is built around.

## Core Distinction by Holding Period

**Swing (2-10 days):** Focus on short-term momentum ignition, volume spikes, and tight consolidation breakouts. Entries are more time-sensitive; exits are rule-based (target/stop), not thesis-based.

**Positional (1 week - 2 months):** Focus on base-building patterns, sustained accumulation, and early-stage trend formation. Allows more room for normal volatility; exits tied to trend continuation/breakdown, not just a fixed target.

## Core Patterns

**1. Volume Dry-Up Before Breakout**
- Price consolidates in a tight range with progressively declining volume over 2-4 weeks — signals seller exhaustion.
- A subsequent volume spike (2x+ average) breaking the range top is a higher-quality signal than a breakout without this prior dry-up.

**2. Base-Building Pattern**
- Extended sideways consolidation (often 4-12 weeks) after a decline, with price stabilizing in a defined range — forms the "base" from which a positional move can launch.
- Higher-quality bases show progressively narrowing range (volatility contraction) rather than continued wide swings.

**3. Momentum Ignition**
- Sharp, high-volume move breaking out of a base or consolidation, often on a specific news/results trigger.
- Distinguish genuine ignition (sustained follow-through over subsequent sessions) from a single-day spike that reverses (common in illiquid small-caps — cross-reference Domain 29).

**4. Sudden Volume Spike Interpretation**
- Volume spike + price up + closing near day's high = bullish accumulation signal.
- Volume spike + price up + closing near day's low (long upper wick) = distribution/selling into strength, a caution signal despite the "up" day.
- Volume spike with no clear direction (doji-like) after a sustained move = potential exhaustion/reversal signal.

**5. Delivery Percentage Confirmation**
- In Indian markets, delivery % (shares actually taken for delivery vs. intraday-squared-off) rising alongside a price move signals genuine investment demand, not just speculative/intraday churn.
- A price breakout on high volume but low delivery % is a weaker, more speculative signal.

**6. Pullback-to-Support Entry (Positional)**
- After a breakout, price often pulls back toward the breakout level (now acting as support) before resuming the trend — this retest, if it holds on lower volume than the breakout, offers a lower-risk entry than chasing the initial breakout.

**7. Relative Strength vs. Sector/Index**
- A stock outperforming its sector index during a broad market pullback signals underlying strength — prioritize such names for positional entries once the broader market stabilizes.
- A stock underperforming during a sector rally is a caution signal even if the standalone chart looks acceptable.

## Application Framework — Entry/Exit Discipline
1. **Define holding-period intent before entry** — a swing setup and a positional setup have different stop-loss and target logic; don't blend them mid-trade.
2. **Require volume confirmation** for any breakout entry — price alone without volume is a weak signal, especially in small/micro-caps (Domain 29).
3. **Pre-define stop-loss and target before entry**, sized appropriately for the holding period (tighter for swing, wider for positional to allow normal volatility).
4. **Never take a technical entry that conflicts with a forensic or governance red flag** (Domain 24, Domain 8) — technical strength cannot substitute for fundamental safety, especially in swing trades where you're relying on the stock to remain tradeable through your holding period.
5. **Scale position sizing to setup quality** — a volume-dry-up + breakout + delivery confirmation setup deserves more size than a single unconfirmed signal.

## Worked Example
A stock consolidates in a ₹95-105 range for 5 weeks with declining daily volume (from 8 lakh shares/day to 3 lakh shares/day) — classic volume dry-up. It then breaks above ₹105 on volume of 18 lakh shares with delivery % rising from 25% to 48%, closing near the day's high. Two sessions later it pulls back to ₹106-108 (former resistance, now support) on volume of just 4 lakh shares before resuming upward. This sequence — dry-up, confirmed breakout, healthy pullback on low volume — is a well-formed positional entry setup, distinctly higher quality than a single-day volume spike with no prior base.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Chasing a breakout on the first day without volume/delivery confirmation, especially in illiquid names.
- Ignoring that a "breakout" occurred on a stock already flagged for forensic or governance concerns.
- Treating every volume spike as bullish — direction of the day's close (near high vs. near low) changes the interpretation entirely.
- Applying swing-trade stop-loss discipline to a positional thesis (getting stopped out on normal volatility) or vice versa (holding a failed swing trade "positionally" after the setup has clearly failed).

## AI Trigger Keywords
breakout, swing trade setup, positional entry, volume spike, base pattern, delivery percentage, support resistance entry, is this a good entry point, momentum stock.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 7 (general technical foundation) · Domain 24/8 (mandatory safety gate before any technical entry) · Domain 29 (liquidity context for small-cap technical entries) · Domain 28 (volume story overlap for multibagger screening).

## Conflict Rule
Technical setup quality never overrides a forensic or governance red flag — a "perfect" breakout chart on a stock with fresh forensic concerns should be passed or, at minimum, sized down sharply and treated as high-risk speculation, not a core setup.

## Universal Rule Applied
Technical analysis improves timing, not business quality (per Domain 7's core rule) — this applies with extra force in swing/positional trading, where the temptation to let a good chart override fundamental caution is highest.

# Domain 31 — Banking Sector Deep Dive
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Adds sub-segment structure, competitive landscape, and stock-picking-specific context for banking beyond the metrics already defined in Domain 26. This domain does not repeat NIM/NPA/CAR formulas — see Domain 26 for those.

## Sub-Segment Structure

**1. Public Sector Banks (PSBs)** — Government-owned majority stake. Characteristics: historically weaker asset quality but improving post-recapitalization cycles, lower valuations (P/B), higher beta to economic cycle, dividend/disinvestment policy sensitivity.

**2. Private Sector Banks (Large)** — Established franchises with strong retail liability franchises. Characteristics: premium valuations justified by consistent ROE, lower credit cost volatility, stronger digital/fee income mix.

**3. Private Sector Banks (Small/Emerging)** — Newer or smaller private banks still building scale. Characteristics: higher growth rates off a smaller base, higher execution risk, more volatile asset quality trends, valuation re-rating potential if scale-up executes well.

**4. Small Finance Banks (SFBs)** — Converted from microfinance/NBFC entities, serve underbanked segments. Characteristics: higher yields but higher credit risk (unsecured/microfinance exposure), typically higher NIM but also higher credit cost, closely watch microfinance-specific stress cycles.

**5. Payment Banks** — Limited banking license (no lending), fee/float-income driven. Niche category, generally not core equity research focus for growth/value investing.

## Competitive Landscape Dynamics
- Digital banking investment intensity is now a competitive differentiator — banks under-investing in digital infrastructure risk losing younger/urban customer acquisition to better-funded peers and fintechs.
- CASA franchise strength (low-cost deposit base) is the most durable moat source in banking — track multi-year CASA ratio trend, not a single quarter.
- Co-lending and fintech partnership models are reshaping how smaller banks access retail/MSME lending growth without proportionate branch expansion.

## Cycle Positioning
- **Credit cycle early-recovery**: asset quality stabilizing, provisioning normalizing, PPOP growth outpacing PAT growth (base effect from lower provisions) — often the best entry window.
- **Credit cycle mid-expansion**: loan growth accelerates, NIMs stable-to-improving, valuations re-rate.
- **Credit cycle late/peak**: aggressive loan growth in riskier segments, competitive pressure on yields, early signs of slippage upticks — caution zone.
- **Credit cycle downturn**: rising slippages, provisioning spikes, PAT volatility — avoid unless deep-value turnaround thesis with strong capital position confirmed.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles (Small/Mid-Cap Banking Focus)
1. **Recapitalized/turnaround PSBs**: watch for sustained (3+ quarter) improvement in slippage ratio and PCR alongside credit growth resumption — early turnaround signal distinct from a one-quarter provisioning write-back.
2. **Emerging private banks scaling branch network**: track cost-to-income ratio trend — should be improving as scale kicks in; a rising cost-to-income despite branch growth signals execution issues.
3. **SFB microfinance stress cycles**: these are sharp and regional (weather/political events can trigger localized collection stress) — monitor state-wise concentration disclosed in investor presentations.
4. **Promoter/management change in smaller private banks**: often a re-rating catalyst if new leadership has a credible turnaround track record (cross-reference Domain 16 Special Situations).

## Worked Example
A small private bank shows GNPA improving from 4.2% to 2.8% over 4 quarters, cost-to-income ratio falling from 58% to 51% as branch network matures, and CASA ratio rising from 28% to 34% — the combination of improving asset quality, operating leverage, and cheaper funding base together justify a P/B re-rating candidate, distinct from a bank showing only one of these three trends improving.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Judging a bank purely on PAT growth without checking whether it's provisioning-driven (lower provisions inflating PAT) vs. genuine PPOP growth.
- Ignoring segment concentration (e.g., heavy microfinance or unsecured retail exposure) that isn't obvious from headline NPA numbers.
- Assuming digital banking investment automatically translates to competitive advantage without evidence of actual customer acquisition/cost benefit.

- Domestic formulation growth tracks broadly with chronic disease prevalence and healthcare spend — structurally steady, less cyclical.

- **Order book size alone is a weak signal** — the key variables are: execution timeline (multi-year contracts inflate headline order book relative to near-term revenue), historical execution track record (has the company delivered past large orders on time), and payment terms (advance payment terms vs. back-ended payment affects working capital).

- **Defence budget allocation timing** — Union Budget capital outlay for defence, and subsequent actual procurement order releases (which can lag budget announcement by quarters), both need tracking — budget headlines are not the same as confirmed orders.

## Competitive Landscape Dynamics
- Indigenization policy (positive list for domestic-only procurement of specified items) is a structural tailwind for private defence manufacturers previously excluded from certain segments — track which platform/component categories move onto restricted-import lists.
- Export orders (increasingly emphasized in policy) diversify revenue away from single-buyer (Government of India) concentration risk — track export order disclosures as a distinct, valuable growth vector.
- Qualification cycles for new private suppliers into defence platforms are long (multi-year trials/certification) — early-stage private players may show promising pipeline but limited near-term revenue conversion.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles (Small/Mid-Cap Defence Focus)
1. **Component suppliers newly qualified for a major platform**: qualification announcement is the catalyst; actual order/revenue conversion typically lags by several quarters — separate the catalyst event from the revenue realization timeline in your thesis.
2. **Book-to-bill trend over 4+ quarters**: sustained >1 ratio alongside improving execution track record is a stronger signal than a single large one-time order announcement.
3. **Export order diversification**: a private manufacturer securing its first meaningful export order (reducing single-customer/government concentration) is a distinct re-rating catalyst worth tracking separately from domestic order flow.

## Worked Example

First export order (to a friendly foreign government, disclosed separately) diversifies customer concentration. This combination — sustained book-to-bill strength + qualification catalyst + export diversification — is a stronger thesis than order-book size alone.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating a large headline "order book" figure as near-term revenue without checking execution timeline (some contracts span 5-10 years).
- Ignoring single-customer concentration risk (Government of India as sole buyer) without evidence of export/private diversification.
- Assuming budget allocation announcements automatically convert to actual procurement orders on the announced timeline — procurement delays are common in this sector.

# Domain 34 — Manufacturing Sector Deep Dive

## Purpose
Adds sub-segment structure, capex-cycle analysis, and stock-picking context for manufacturing beyond Domain 12's one-line coverage. "Manufacturing" spans diverse sub-industries (capital goods overlaps separately in Domain 12); this domain focuses on general/diversified manufacturing analytical discipline applicable across sub-sectors.

## Sub-Segment Structure

**1. Contract/Job-Work Manufacturing** — Manufactures per customer specification/design (auto ancillary, electronics assembly-type models). Characteristics: revenue tied directly to customer's own volume, lower pricing power but steadier demand if diversified across customers, margin depends on operational efficiency more than brand/product differentiation.

**2. Own-Brand/Product Manufacturing** — Company designs, manufactures, and sells its own branded products. Characteristics: higher margin potential, requires distribution/brand investment, more exposed to demand-side marketing execution risk.

**3. Engineering/Capital Equipment Manufacturing** — Machinery, industrial equipment for other manufacturers. Characteristics: order-book driven (similar dynamics to Capital Goods in Domain 12), long sales cycles, aftermarket/spares revenue often higher-margin and more stable than new equipment sales.

**4. Process/Continuous Manufacturing** — Chemicals, cement-adjacent, continuous-process industries. Characteristics: high fixed-cost base, capacity utilization is the dominant profitability driver, scale economics matter significantly.

## Capex Cycle Analysis (Core Skill for This Sector)
- **New capacity announcement → commissioning → ramp-up → full utilization** is the standard cycle; each stage has different investment implications:
  - Announcement stage: thesis is forward-looking, execution risk is unverified — lower conviction warranted.
  - Commissioning stage: watch for delays vs. original guided timeline — repeated delays are a management execution red flag.
  - Ramp-up stage: watch utilization trajectory quarter-by-quarter — smooth ramp-up vs. stalled utilization signals different outcomes.
  - Full utilization stage: operating leverage should now be visible in margins; this is typically where the re-rating opportunity (if any) has already partly played out.
- **Debt-funded vs. internal-accrual-funded capex**: debt-funded expansion raises financial risk if ramp-up is delayed (interest cost accrues regardless of utilization) — cross-reference Domain 4/9 leverage and coverage ratios.
- **Capacity utilization benchmarks vary by industry** — compare against sub-sector-specific historical utilization ranges, not a generic threshold.

## Competitive Landscape Dynamics
- Customer/product concentration is a common vulnerability in contract manufacturing — a company overly dependent on one large customer faces both pricing pressure risk and revenue cliff risk if that relationship ends.
- Raw material cost pass-through ability varies significantly — companies with formula-linked pricing contracts (automatic pass-through) are structurally lower-risk than those absorbing input cost volatility into margin.
- Export-oriented manufacturers benefit from China+1 diversification but face currency and global-demand cyclicality that domestic-focused manufacturers don't.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles (Small/Mid-Cap Manufacturing Focus)
1. **Capacity expansion with pre-secured demand**: expansions backed by disclosed long-term customer contracts/LOIs are meaningfully lower-risk than speculative capacity addition — verify whether demand visibility exists before capex, not just capex announcement.
2. **Utilization inflection tracking**: a company moving from 60% to 75%+ utilization on existing assets (before new capacity even comes online) is strong operating-leverage evidence — track this via disclosed utilization commentary or back-calculated from revenue/installed capacity.
3. **Customer diversification progress**: a contract manufacturer reducing dependency on its top 1-2 customers (disclosed customer concentration trend in annual report) reduces a key structural risk and can support re-rating.
4. **Import substitution manufacturing**: domestic manufacturers displacing imports in a specific product category (often policy-supported) — verify via declining import trend data or explicit customer disclosure of import replacement.

## Worked Example
A specialty component manufacturer's capacity utilization rose from 55% to 78% over 6 quarters purely from existing asset base (no new capex yet), with EBITDA margin expanding from 11% to 16% over the same period — clear operating leverage. Customer concentration (top 3 customers) fell from 65% of revenue to 48% as two new OEM relationships matured. New capacity (funded 70% via internal accruals, 30% debt) is now commissioning with pre-secured off-take agreements disclosed for roughly half of the new capacity. This combination of demonstrated operating leverage + reducing concentration risk + de-risked new capex is a stronger thesis than capacity expansion alone.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating a capex announcement itself as the investment thesis without evidence of demand visibility or execution track record.
- Ignoring customer concentration disclosed in annual report notes when assessing revenue durability.
- Assuming capacity utilization improvement is permanent without checking whether it's cyclical (industry-wide demand upcycle) vs. company-specific market share gain.

# Domain 35 — REIT & InvIT Sector Deep Dive
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain — no prior coverage existed)

## Purpose
REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts) and InvITs (Infrastructure Investment Trusts) are structurally distinct instruments from regular equity — different regulatory structure, distribution mandate, and valuation logic. This domain fills a complete gap; no prior domain covered this instrument type.

## Structural Basics
- **REIT**: Owns and operates income-generating real estate (primarily commercial office, retail malls); mandated to distribute a high % of Net Distributable Cash Flow (NDCF) to unit holders.
- **InvIT**: Owns and operates infrastructure assets (roads, power transmission, pipelines, telecom towers); similar distribution mandate structure.
- Both are regulated under SEBI-specific regulations distinct from standard equity listing rules, with mandated minimum distribution frequency (typically not less than twice a year) and leverage ceiling caps.

## Core Metrics (Distinct from Standard Equity Analysis)

**1. Net Distributable Cash Flow (NDCF)** — The cash flow mandated to be distributed to unit holders; the primary "earnings" metric to focus on, not accounting PAT.

**2. Distribution Yield** — Annual distribution per unit / current unit price. The primary valuation anchor, analogous to dividend yield but structurally central (not incidental) to the instrument.

**3. Occupancy Rate (REIT)** — % of leasable area actually leased; trend and re-leasing spread (rent achieved on renewal vs. expiring rent) are key indicators of underlying asset quality and market strength.

**4. Weighted Average Lease Expiry (WALE)** — Average remaining lease tenure across the portfolio, weighted by rental value; longer WALE = more revenue visibility/stability, shorter WALE = more re-leasing/rollover risk but also more upside capture potential if market rents are rising.

**5. Net Asset Value (NAV) per Unit** — Independent valuer's assessment of underlying property/infrastructure asset value per unit; compare current unit price to NAV for premium/discount assessment (similar logic to Domain 5's asset value method).

**6. Loan-to-Value (LTV) / Leverage** — SEBI caps leverage for REITs/InvITs; track against the regulatory ceiling and assess refinancing risk given the interest-rate environment (Domain 1).

**7. Asset Acquisition Pipeline** — Growth for REITs/InvITs comes primarily through acquiring additional yield-generating assets (not organic same-store growth alone) — track disclosed pipeline and funding plan (debt vs. fresh unit issuance) for accretion/dilution impact.

## Application Framework
1. **Distribution yield vs. bond yield comparison**: REITs/InvITs are often analyzed relative to the risk-free rate (G-Sec yield, Domain 1) plus a spread for illiquidity/asset risk — a compressing spread (yield falling toward risk-free rate) signals rich valuation, a widening spread signals cheap valuation or rising perceived risk.
2. **WALE and occupancy trend together**: rising occupancy with shortening WALE (more near-term lease rollovers) has different risk/reward than stable occupancy with long WALE — assess both together, not in isolation.
3. **New unit issuance for acquisitions**: assess whether the acquisition is NDCF-accretive per unit after the dilution from fresh unit issuance — similar arithmetic discipline to Domain 23's M&A accretion/dilution check.
4. **Interest rate sensitivity**: REITs/InvITs behave partly like bond-proxies — rising rate environments typically pressure valuations (competing yield from risk-free instruments rises) even if underlying asset performance (occupancy, rents) is stable.

## Worked Example
A REIT trades at a 6.2% distribution yield while the 10-year G-Sec yield is 7.0% — a negative spread, suggesting the market is pricing in either strong future rental growth or accepting compressed compensation for illiquidity/asset risk; this deserves scrutiny of the re-leasing spread and occupancy trend before assuming the valuation is justified. If re-leasing spreads have been consistently positive (say +8-10% on renewals) and WALE remains healthy at 6+ years, the growth-embedded thesis has support; if re-leasing spreads are flat/negative, the current yield may not be adequately compensating for risk.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Comparing REIT/InvIT unit price purely on absolute price without reference to distribution yield or NAV — these instruments are yield/asset-value driven, not growth-multiple driven like typical equities.
- Ignoring near-term lease/contract expiry concentration (a large % of WALE expiring in the same year) as a rollover risk.
- Debt-funded acquisitions pushing leverage toward the regulatory ceiling without adequate NDCF accretion to justify the added financial risk.
- Assuming distribution yield is guaranteed/fixed like a bond coupon — NDCF can fluctuate with occupancy, rental collection, and asset performance.

## AI Trigger Keywords
REIT, InvIT, distribution yield, NDCF, occupancy rate, WALE, real estate investment trust, infrastructure investment trust, rental yield stock.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 1 (interest rate sensitivity, bond-proxy behavior) · Domain 5 (NAV-based valuation logic parallel) · Domain 20 (yield/spread comparison framework) · Domain 9 (Liquidity Risk — REIT/InvIT units can be less liquid than large-cap equity).

## Conflict Rule
Distribution yield spread over risk-free rate governs valuation attractiveness more than unit price trend alone — a rising unit price with a compressing/negative yield spread is a caution signal, not confirmation of value.

## Universal Rule Applied
NDCF and distribution figures must be sourced from the REIT/InvIT's mandated disclosures (which differ in format from standard equity financial statements) — never conflate reported accounting profit with distributable cash flow for this instrument class.

## Sub-Segment Structure

**1. Life Insurance** — Long-duration savings/protection products. Characteristics: embedded value and VNB margin are the core valuation drivers (Domain 26); product mix (protection vs. savings/ULIP) heavily affects margin quality — pure protection has higher margins but smaller ticket sizes, savings-linked products have lower margins but larger premium volumes.

**2. General Insurance (Non-Life)** — Motor, health, property, fire, marine, etc. Characteristics: combined ratio (Domain 26) is the key underwriting profitability metric; motor and health are typically the largest and most competitive segments; catastrophe/weather-event exposure creates episodic volatility distinct from life insurance's smoother profile.

**3. Health Insurance (Standalone)** — Dedicated health insurers. Characteristics: claims ratio trend is critical (medical inflation running structurally above general inflation pressures loss ratios over time); distribution mix (agency vs. bancassurance vs. digital) affects customer acquisition cost and persistency.

**4. Reinsurance** — Insures the insurers; niche, largely institutional-scale segment in India, limited pure-play listed options.

## Product Mix & Margin Quality (Life Insurance Specific)
- Pure protection (term plans) — highest VNB margin, smallest premium contribution, most "insurance-like" and least "investment-like."
- Non-par savings — moderate margin, guaranteed-return products; margin sensitive to interest rate environment (long-duration liability matching).
- ULIP (Unit Linked Insurance Plans) — lower margin, more investment-linked, sensitive to equity market sentiment for sales volume.
- **A rising share of protection/non-par in the product mix generally signals improving margin quality** — track this mix shift over time rather than looking at NBP growth alone.

## Distribution Channel Analysis
- **Agency channel**: higher control over persistency/quality but higher fixed cost; traditional insurers' backbone.
- **Bancassurance**: leverages bank branch networks for distribution — insurers tied to strong bank partners (especially if promoter-linked) benefit from lower acquisition cost and access, but are also dependent on that banking relationship continuing on favorable terms.
- **Digital/direct channel**: growing but still smaller contributor; lower acquisition cost per policy but historically mixed persistency track record — monitor as it scales.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles
1. **VNB margin trend combined with product mix shift**: a rising VNB margin driven by genuine mix shift toward protection/non-par is higher quality than one driven by a one-time assumption change (economic/operating assumptions can be adjusted to flatter margins — check embedded value operating variance disclosures for genuine vs. assumption-driven margin change).
2. **Persistency trend by cohort**: 13th-month persistency (early lapse behavior) vs. 61st-month persistency (long-term retention) tell different stories — a company improving 13th-month persistency is fixing acquisition/underwriting quality; 61st-month persistency reflects longer-term product/service satisfaction.
3. **General insurance combined ratio trend by segment**: motor and health often have divergent trends — a blended combined ratio improvement driven by one segment while another deteriorates needs segment-level disclosure to assess sustainably.
4. **Bancassurance dependency risk**: an insurer heavily reliant on a single bank partner for distribution faces concentration risk if that relationship terms change or the partner bank pushes a competing insurer's products.

## Worked Example
A life insurer shows VNB margin improving from 24% to 28% over 2 years. Checking the drivers: protection mix rose from 8% to 13% of APE (Annualized Premium Equivalent), and non-par savings rose from 20% to 27%, while ULIP share fell correspondingly — a genuine mix-shift-driven margin improvement, not an assumption change (confirmed via the embedded value walk disclosure showing minimal "assumption change" variance line item). This is meaningfully higher-quality than margin improvement driven primarily by a discount rate or persistency assumption revision.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Accepting VNB margin improvement at face value without checking the embedded value operating variance breakdown for assumption-driven vs. genuine operational improvement.
- Ignoring bancassurance channel concentration risk in insurers heavily dependent on a single banking partner.
- In general insurance, a falling combined ratio driven by under-provisioning for claims (rather than genuine underwriting improvement) — cross-reference reserve adequacy commentary and any reserve strengthening/releases disclosed.

## Universal Rule Applied
Embedded value and VNB figures involve significant actuarial judgment — always disclose when a margin or EV trend cannot be decomposed into assumption-change vs. genuine operating improvement due to insufficient disclosure detail.

Characteristics: volume-driven, margin sensitive to delivery density (higher density = better unit economics), intensely competitive with e-commerce platforms sometimes building in-house capability (disintermediation risk for pure third-party players).

- **GST-driven warehouse consolidation**: post-GST, companies increasingly consolidate warehousing into fewer, larger, more efficient hubs rather than state-wise fragmented storage — track large-format warehousing capacity growth as a structural indicator.
- **E-commerce logistics demand**: structural growth driver, but increasingly captured in-house by large e-commerce platforms — assess whether a third-party logistics player has differentiated capability (cold chain, specialized handling, geographic reach) that resists disintermediation.
- **DFC (Dedicated Freight Corridor) commissioning**: progressive commissioning shifts long-haul freight economics toward rail — logistics companies with rail-linked capability stand to benefit disproportionately as sections complete.
- **Fuel cost pass-through**: assess whether contracts have fuel cost escalation clauses (structurally lower-risk) vs. fixed-rate contracts absorbing fuel volatility into margin.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles (Small/Mid-Cap Logistics Focus)
1. **Asset-light vs. asset-heavy model distinction**: asset-light aggregators show better ROCE/scalability but more competitive intensity; asset-heavy players have higher entry barriers but higher capital intensity and cyclical earnings — match the model to the specific investment thesis (growth/scalability vs. steady cash generation).
2. **Warehousing occupancy and contract tenure**: similar logic to Domain 35's REIT framework — long-tenure corporate contracts provide revenue visibility; track occupancy trend and any large client concentration.
3. **DFC-linked rail logistics beneficiaries**: companies with rail-siding access or dedicated rail logistics capability positioned to capture volume shift as DFC sections commission — verify actual operational readiness, not just thematic exposure.
4. **Cold chain specialization**: niche players serving pharma/high-value perishables command better pricing and stickier contracts than generic cold storage — differentiate specialized vs. commodity cold chain exposure.

## Worked Example
A warehousing logistics company shows large-format warehouse capacity growing from 2 million sq ft to 3.5 million sq ft over 2 years, with average contract tenure with corporate clients at 5+ years and top-5 client concentration falling from 55% to 40% of revenue as new clients are added. - Assuming e-commerce logistics exposure is automatically a durable tailwind without checking disintermediation risk from platforms building in-house capability.
- Ignoring fuel cost pass-through terms in freight/logistics contracts — fixed-rate contracts in a rising fuel cost environment compress margins materially.
- Treating "asset-light" as automatically superior without recognizing the correspondingly lower barriers to entry and higher competitive intensity in that model.

# Domain 38 — Power Sector Deep Dive
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Adds sub-segment structure and stock-picking context for power beyond Domain 12's one-line coverage (PLF, tariff structure, fuel pass-through, regulatory asset base). Also complements Domain 12's separate Renewable Energy entry with power-sector-wide integration context.

## Sub-Segment Structure

**1. Thermal Generation (Coal/Gas)** — Base-load power generation. Characteristics: PLF (Plant Load Factor) trend is the key operating metric; fuel cost pass-through mechanism (regulated tariff vs. merchant/market-linked sale) determines margin stability; coal linkage security (domestic coal supply agreements vs. imported coal dependency) is a structural risk differentiator.

**2. Renewable Generation (Solar/Wind)** — Covered separately in Domain 12 with capacity addition/PLF/tariff metrics; this domain adds integration context: renewable capacity's variable output requires either grid-balancing (storage/thermal backup) or long-term PPA structures to monetize reliably — assess a renewable player's PPA quality (counterparty credit strength, tariff lock-in duration) as much as capacity growth.

**3. Transmission** — High-voltage power transmission infrastructure (often regulated, tariff-based revenue via CERC/SERC-approved Regulated Asset Base returns). Characteristics: highly stable, annuity-like cash flows once assets are commissioned; primary risk is project execution/commissioning delay, not demand risk, since transmission tariffs are typically availability-based (paid for making capacity available, not for actual power flow).

**4. Distribution (Discoms)** — Power distribution to end consumers. Characteristics: predominantly state-government-owned with historically weak financial health (AT&C losses — Aggregate Technical & Commercial losses — a key metric); private distribution licensees in select cities are a distinct, generally healthier sub-category; discom financial stress is a sector-wide risk factor affecting payment timeliness to generators.

**5. Power Trading** — Intermediaries facilitating power purchase/sale between generators and distributors/consumers, including exchange-based trading. Characteristics: volume and margin-per-unit driven, benefits from growing short-term/merchant power market activity.

**6. Power Equipment & EPC** — Manufacturers/contractors for generation, transmission, and distribution equipment. Characteristics: order-book driven similar to Capital Goods (Domain 12); benefits from both conventional grid strengthening capex and the broader renewable/transmission buildout.

## Key Structural Considerations

**Regulatory Asset Base (RAB) Model** — For regulated utilities (many transmission and some distribution entities), returns are calculated on the approved asset base at a regulator-determined rate — understanding the applicable regulatory order and approved return rate is essential, as it caps upside but also provides downside stability distinct from merchant power price volatility.

**Discom Payment Risk** — Generators selling to financially weak state discoms face receivable/payment delay risk regardless of their own operational efficiency — track receivable days specifically from state utility counterparties as a distinct risk indicator from overall company receivables.

**PPA (Power Purchase Agreement) Quality** — For any generation asset (thermal or renewable), the PPA counterparty's credit quality, tariff structure (fixed vs. escalating vs. merchant-exposed), and remaining tenure materially determine cash flow predictability — a renewable asset with a strong central PSU counterparty PPA is structurally safer than one selling to a weak state discom or on merchant/spot basis.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles
1. **Coal linkage security for thermal generators**: assess whether domestic coal supply is secured via long-term linkage vs. dependent on e-auction/imported coal spot purchases — the latter carries higher fuel cost volatility risk.
2. **Renewable PPA counterparty quality**: differentiate renewable developers selling primarily to strong counterparties (central PSU utilities, strong private consumers via open access) from those heavily exposed to weak state discoms.
3. **Transmission asset commissioning pipeline**: track under-construction transmission projects and expected commissioning dates — since revenue begins upon commissioning with high certainty (RAB-based), commissioning timeline is the primary near-term catalyst to track, not demand uncertainty.
4. **AT&C loss reduction trajectory (Discom-linked plays)**: for companies exposed to discom health (equipment suppliers, generators with discom-heavy PPA books), track state-wise AT&C loss trends as a leading indicator of counterparty payment risk.

## Worked Example
A renewable energy developer's capacity grew from 500 MW to 900 MW over 2 years, but examining the PPA book shows 60% is with a strong central PSU utility at fixed escalating tariffs with 20+ year tenure, while 40% is merchant/short-term exposure to state discoms with a history of payment delays. Receivable days from the discom-linked portion are running 180+ days vs. under 45 days for the PSU-linked portion. Capacity growth alone would look uniformly positive, but the PPA quality decomposition reveals meaningfully different cash flow risk within the same "capacity growth" headline.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Evaluating renewable/thermal capacity growth without decomposing PPA counterparty quality — not all "contracted capacity" carries equal cash flow certainty.
- Ignoring receivable days specifically from state discom counterparties, which can be materially worse than a company's blended average.
- Assuming regulatory asset base returns are risk-free — regulatory order changes/tariff petition outcomes can alter approved return rates.

power stock, thermal generation, renewable energy stock, transmission company, discom, PPA quality, plant load factor, AT&C losses, power trading.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 12 (base demand drivers, renewable energy metrics) · Domain 22 (CERC/SERC regulatory framework) · Domain 20 (receivable/credit risk from discom counterparties) · Domain 9 (Regulatory Risk).

## Conflict Rule
PPA/counterparty quality overrides headline capacity growth for any generation asset — larger capacity with weaker counterparty exposure is not automatically a stronger thesis than smaller capacity with strong counterparties.

## Universal Rule Applied
PPA tenure, tariff structure, and counterparty identity should be sourced from disclosed contract summaries/investor presentations, with explicit disclosure when counterparty credit quality cannot be independently verified.

Characteristics: order-driven by Indian Railways' capex budget and modernization programs (e.g., Vande Bharat-type programs); private participation has been expanding from a historically railway-PSU-dominated supply base.

**2. Signaling & Train Control Systems** — Safety and traffic management technology (automatic train protection, signaling modernization). **4. Dedicated Freight Corridor (DFC) Beneficiaries** — Companies benefiting from DFC commissioning (both direct EPC contractors and downstream logistics beneficiaries — cross-reference Domain 37 Logistics). Characteristics: DFC progressively shifts freight economics toward rail; beneficiaries include rail logistics operators, container corporation-type entities, and industries with high rail-freight dependency (bulk commodities: coal, steel, cement).

**5. Station Redevelopment & Real Estate-Linked** — Railway station modernization/redevelopment programs, often involving private participation in commercial real estate development around stations. Characteristics: longer-gestation, real-estate-linked economics distinct from pure rail-operations plays.

**6. Ancillary/Component Suppliers** — Component manufacturers supplying rolling stock/infrastructure builders (couplers, brake systems, electrical components). Characteristics: similar dynamics to Auto Ancillary (Domain 12) — content-per-unit and customer diversification matter.

- **Indian Railways capex cycle**: Union Budget allocation to Railways (capital outlay) is the primary demand driver for rolling stock/infrastructure suppliers — track budget allocation trend and, more importantly, actual tender/order releases which can lag budget announcements.
- **Private participation expansion**: policy has progressively opened rolling stock manufacturing and select operations to private players (previously railway-PSU-dominated) — track which specific product/service categories are opening to private tender.
- **DFC commissioning progress**: phased commissioning of Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors structurally increases rail's freight modal share versus road — direct EPC beneficiaries capture near-term order value, while logistics/freight operators capture longer-term structural volume benefit (see Domain 37).
- **Safety modernization mandate**: post-incident regulatory push for advanced signaling/train protection systems creates demand somewhat independent of the general capex cycle — a distinct, policy-mandate-driven revenue stream for signaling suppliers.

## Stock-Picking Specific Angles (Small/Mid-Cap Railway-Linked Focus)
1. **Order book quality from Indian Railways tenders**: similar discipline to Domain 33 (Defence) — assess execution timeline and historical track record of on-time delivery to Indian Railways, not just headline order value; Railways contracts often have strict penalty clauses for delays.
2. **Private rolling stock manufacturer scale-up**: companies newly qualified to supply categories previously railway-PSU-exclusive represent a structural opportunity — verify actual qualification/certification status via exchange filings, not just intent.
3. **Signaling technology differentiation**: prioritize suppliers with proprietary/differentiated signaling technology (higher margin, less commoditized) over generic track-laying/civil contractors (lower margin, more competitive, execution-risk-heavy).
4. **DFC-linked component/logistics plays**: cross-reference Domain 37 for the downstream logistics beneficiary angle; for direct EPC/component suppliers to DFC construction, track project-specific commissioning milestones as the catalyst timeline.

## Worked Example
A signaling systems supplier secures a multi-year contract for automatic train protection system rollout across a large rail network segment, mandated under a safety modernization policy. The order is backed by a specific government safety directive (verifiable via official notification), giving it lower "will this get delayed/cancelled" risk than a discretionary capex-linked order. Execution track record on two prior smaller signaling contracts shows on-time delivery. This combination — policy-mandated (not just budget-discretionary) demand, verifiable directive, and clean execution history — is a stronger thesis than a generic track-laying EPC order of similar headline value.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating all "railway order" headlines as equally reliable — mandate-driven safety orders differ meaningfully from discretionary capex orders in cancellation/delay risk.
- Ignoring Indian Railways' penalty clause structure for delayed execution, which can materially affect margins on delayed projects.
- Assuming DFC-linked thesis timing without verifying actual section-wise commissioning status against official progress updates (Domain 17 government sources).

# Domain 40 — Screening Strategies
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Concrete, actionable screening criteria combinations to *find* stock candidates matching each stock-finder type referenced throughout this library (multibagger, turnaround, SIP-growth, volume-story) — this domain is the practical "how do I generate a watchlist" layer that Domain 28 assumes as input.

## Core Principle
A screen generates candidates for research, never a final decision. Every screen output must pass through Domain 24 (Forensic) before further work.

## Screen 1: Early Multibagger Screen
- Market cap: micro/small-cap range (below your defined ceiling)
- Revenue/PAT CAGR (3-yr): accelerating, not just positive (latest year growth > 3-yr average growth)
- Piotroski F-Score: ≥6
- Promoter holding: stable or increasing over last 4 quarters
- Promoter pledge: nil or near-nil
- Fresh smart-money entry (Domain 27) in last 1-2 quarters: optional but high-value filter
- Debt/Equity: reasonable for the sector (not distressed)

## Screen 2: Turnaround Screen
- PAT: negative or depressed in trailing 2-3 years, now showing sequential (QoQ) improvement
- Debt trend: declining over last 3-4 quarters
- Interest coverage ratio: improving sequentially
- Management change OR clear disclosed corrective action in last 12-18 months
- New auditor/no adverse audit remarks in most recent report
- Promoter pledge: declining trend (if previously elevated) — rising pledge disqualifies

## Screen 3: SIP-Growth / Compounder Screen
- Revenue/PAT CAGR (5-yr): consistent 15-25%+, low year-to-year volatility
- ROE/ROCE: consistently >15% for 5+ years
- Debt/Equity: low, stable or declining
- Dividend payout: moderate (not near-zero, not excessive) — signals capital allocation balance (cross-reference Domain 41)
- Piotroski F-Score: ≥7
- Promoter holding: stable/increasing, pledge nil

## Screen 4: Volume Story / Uptrend Screen (Technical Overlay)
- Price: above key moving averages (e.g., 50-day and 200-day)
- Volume: recent average significantly above 3-6 month average (1.5x+)
- Delivery %: rising alongside volume (Domain 30)
- 52-week high proximity: trading within a defined % of 52-week high (momentum confirmation)
- **Must be paired with Screen 1 or 3 fundamentals** — pure technical screens without fundamental backing are higher-risk speculative trades, not investment candidates

## Screen 5: Valuation-SIP (Quality-at-Reasonable-Price) Screen
- PEG ratio: <1.5 (growth-adjusted value)
- P/E: below 5-year historical average band
- ROE: >15%
- Forensic screen: clean (mandatory)
- Sector-relative valuation: below peer average multiple despite comparable/better fundamentals (cross-reference Domain 12 industry benchmarks)

## Application Framework — Combining Screens
1. Run fundamental screens (1, 2, 3, or 5) first to generate the candidate list — never start from a pure technical/volume screen alone.
2. Apply Domain 24 (Forensic) as a mandatory second-pass filter — eliminate any candidate with 2+ red flags regardless of how well it scored on the primary screen.
3. Layer Domain 27 (Super-Investor) as a confidence booster, not a primary filter — its absence doesn't disqualify a candidate, but its presence raises research priority.
4. Apply Screen 4 (technical) only at the entry-timing stage for candidates that already passed fundamental + forensic screens — never as the primary discovery method.
5. Re-run screens periodically (quarterly, aligned with results season) — a candidate's screen status changes as new financials are disclosed.

## Worked Example
Running Screen 1 (Early Multibagger) on a watchlist of 200 small-caps yields 14 candidates with accelerating growth and clean pledge status. Applying the Domain 24 forensic filter eliminates 5 (2 with declining CFO/PAT ratio, 3 with recent auditor changes). Of the remaining 9, cross-checking Domain 27 shows 2 have fresh smart-money entries in the last quarter — these become top-priority for deep-dive fundamental research (Domain 6, Domain 28), while the other 7 remain a secondary research queue.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating a screen pass as a buy signal — screens generate research candidates only.
- Over-optimizing screen thresholds to match a specific stock you already like (confirmation bias, Domain 11) rather than applying consistent criteria across the universe.
- Running only Screen 4 (technical/volume) without fundamental screening — this produces momentum-chasing candidates, not genuine multibagger/turnaround candidates.
- Not re-running screens regularly — a stock's fundamental screen status can deteriorate quickly, especially in small-caps.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 24 (mandatory forensic second-pass) · Domain 27 (smart-money confidence layer) · Domain 28 (deep-dive framework for screen output) · Domain 30 (technical entry timing for passed candidates).

## Conflict Rule
Fundamental + forensic screen results override any pure technical/volume screen signal in determining research priority — a technically exciting stock that fails the fundamental/forensic screen should not be elevated to a research candidate based on chart pattern alone.

## Universal Rule Applied
Screening criteria and thresholds should be applied consistently across the full universe being screened, not selectively adjusted to justify a pre-selected stock — disclose the exact criteria used for any screen output presented.

# Domain 41 — Dividend Analysis
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Standalone dividend framework beyond Domain 4's single yield formula — covers sustainability assessment, payout quality, and how dividend behavior signals capital allocation discipline (relevant to Domain 6/15 quality assessment and Domain 40's SIP-growth screen).

## Core Metrics

**1. Dividend Yield** = Dividend per Share / Market Price (defined in Domain 4; repeated here only as anchor)

**2. Dividend Payout Ratio** = Dividends Paid / Net Profit — the % of earnings distributed vs. retained for reinvestment.

**3. Dividend Coverage Ratio** = Net Profit / Dividends Paid (inverse of payout ratio) — higher coverage signals more cushion to sustain the dividend even if earnings dip.

**4. Free Cash Flow Payout Ratio** = Dividends Paid / Free Cash Flow — arguably more important than the earnings-based payout ratio, since dividends are ultimately paid from cash, not accounting profit (cross-reference Domain 2).

**5. Dividend Growth Rate** — Year-on-year or CAGR growth in dividend per share; consistency of growth (vs. volatility) signals management confidence in earnings durability.

## Payout Philosophy Spectrum

| Payout Level | Characteristics | Typical Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Very Low (0-15%) | Heavy reinvestment, high growth runway assumed | Early-stage growth/multibagger candidates (Domain 28) |
| Moderate (15-40%) | Balance of growth reinvestment and shareholder return | SIP-growth/compounder candidates (Domain 40 Screen 3) |
| High (40-70%) | Mature, moderate growth, cash-generative | Stable large-caps, steady dividend plays |
| Very High (70%+) | Limited reinvestment opportunity, or distribution-mandated structure | Utilities, REITs/InvITs (Domain 35), mature/declining growth businesses |

## Dividend Sustainability Assessment
1. **FCF payout ratio trend** — a rising FCF payout ratio (dividends consuming a growing share of free cash flow) over multiple years signals the dividend may not be sustainable at current levels without either earnings growth or eventual cuts.
2. **Debt-funded dividends** — check whether dividend payments coincide with rising debt or declining cash reserves; a company borrowing to sustain dividend payout is a red flag (cross-reference Domain 9 Financial Risk).
3. **Earnings volatility vs. dividend stability** — a company maintaining stable/growing dividends through earnings volatility either has strong reserves or is at risk of an eventual cut; check reserve levels and payout ratio trend during the weak-earnings period specifically.
4. **Special/one-time dividends** — distinguish from regular recurring dividends; a large one-time special dividend (often from asset sale proceeds) is not a repeatable signal of ongoing capital allocation policy.

## Dividend as a Capital Allocation Quality Signal
- **Rising payout alongside rising ROE/ROCE**: healthy — company is returning cash it doesn't need for value-accretive reinvestment while maintaining strong returns on retained capital.
- **Rising payout alongside declining ROE/ROCE**: caution — may signal the company is running out of good reinvestment opportunities (maturing business) or, worse, is prioritizing distribution over value-accretive growth capex.
- **Low/no payout with high ROE/ROCE and strong growth**: appropriate for genuine growth-stage companies — cross-reference Domain 15 (capital allocation discipline) to assess whether retained earnings are actually being deployed at high returns, not just hoarded.
- **Low/no payout with mediocre ROE/ROCE**: a red flag — capital is neither being returned nor being deployed productively.

## Application Framework
1. Never evaluate dividend yield in isolation — always pair with payout ratio and FCF coverage to assess sustainability.
2. For SIP-growth/compounder candidates (Domain 40 Screen 3), treat moderate, growing payout as a positive quality signal, not the primary selection criterion.
3. For REIT/InvIT analysis (Domain 35), apply the NDCF-based distribution framework instead of standard equity dividend logic — the two are not directly comparable.
4. Flag any dividend cut or suspension as a significant signal requiring investigation into the underlying cause (temporary cash conservation vs. structural earnings deterioration).

## Worked Example
A company has raised its dividend per share every year for 8 years (headline looks attractive), but FCF payout ratio has risen from 35% to 78% over the same period while ROCE declined from 22% to 14% — the dividend growth streak is being sustained by consuming an increasing share of a shrinking cash flow base and declining returns, not by genuine earnings strength. This is a sustainability red flag masked by a superficially attractive "consistent dividend growth" narrative.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Chasing high dividend yield without checking whether it's sustainable (high yield often results from a falling stock price, not an attractive payout).
- Treating a dividend growth streak as automatically positive without checking FCF coverage and underlying ROE/ROCE trend.
- Confusing a one-time special dividend with a recurring capital allocation policy.
- Ignoring debt-funded dividend payments as a warning sign.

## AI Trigger Keywords
dividend yield, dividend stock, payout ratio, dividend growth, is this dividend safe, dividend sustainability, high dividend yield stock.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 4 (yield formula base) · Domain 2 (FCF as the true funding source) · Domain 6/15 (capital allocation quality signal) · Domain 35 (REIT/InvIT distribution framework, distinct logic) · Domain 9 (debt-funded dividend as Financial Risk).

## Conflict Rule
FCF payout ratio and ROE/ROCE trend override headline dividend yield or dividend growth streak when they diverge — an attractive yield or growth streak funded by deteriorating cash flow/returns is a caution signal, not a quality signal.

## Universal Rule Applied
Dividend sustainability conclusions must be grounded in FCF and balance sheet trend data (Domain 2), not accepted from a dividend growth streak alone — always disclose the FCF payout ratio trend alongside any dividend yield/growth discussion.

# Domain 42 — Scuttlebutt Research
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Ground-level qualitative research methodology (Philip Fisher's "scuttlebutt" approach) — gathering information from the ecosystem around a company (customers, suppliers, competitors, employees, dealers) to validate or challenge conclusions drawn from financial statements alone. Especially valuable for small/micro-caps where formal analyst coverage and disclosure depth are limited.

## Core Principle
Financial statements tell you what happened; scuttlebutt research helps you understand why, and whether it's likely to continue. This is corroborating evidence, not a replacement for the financial/forensic screens (Domain 2-4, 24) — always used alongside, not instead of.

## Information Sources & Methods

**1. Customer/Channel Checks**
- For consumer-facing companies: visit retail outlets, check product availability, shelf space, pricing versus competitors, and staff/dealer sentiment about the brand's momentum.
- For B2B companies: understanding customer concentration and satisfaction through indirect channels (industry forums, trade publications, customer's own disclosures about supplier relationships).

**2. Supplier/Vendor Checks**
- Understanding a company's payment discipline and relationship quality from the supplier side can reveal working capital stress or strength not fully visible in headline financials.
- Supplier concentration and dependency patterns can reveal both the company's and its suppliers' relative bargaining power.

**3. Competitor Intelligence**
- Competitor investor presentations, annual reports, and management commentary often reference competitive dynamics (market share commentary, pricing trends) that corroborate or challenge the subject company's own narrative.
- Comparing capacity expansion plans across competitors reveals whether an entire sub-segment is entering an overcapacity risk phase.

**4. Industry/Trade Association Data**
- Sector-specific trade body data (production volumes, capacity utilization, export/import trends) often provides earlier or more granular signals than company-specific quarterly disclosure.
- Cross-reference Domain 12 industry-specific metrics and Domain 17 government/industry data sources.

**5. Employee & Management Turnover Signals**
- Unusual senior management/CFO turnover, especially unexplained or rapid succession, is a qualitative red flag worth investigating alongside Domain 8 (Governance) and Domain 24 (Forensic) quantitative signals.
- LinkedIn/public professional network changes (aggregate patterns, not individual stalking) can sometimes reveal organizational stability trends.

**6. Physical/Digital Presence Verification**
- For manufacturing companies: satellite imagery (where accessible) or public information about facility status can help verify claimed capacity expansion progress independent of company announcements.
- For consumer/retail companies: app store reviews, social media sentiment, and e-commerce platform ratings/reviews provide a real-time proxy for brand health between quarterly disclosures.

**7. Regulatory & Legal Record Checks**
- Court records, regulatory show-cause notices, and litigation history (where publicly searchable) can reveal disputes or compliance issues not prominently featured in company disclosures.

## Application Framework
1. Use scuttlebutt research to validate specific, falsifiable claims from the investment thesis — e.g., "capacity utilization is improving" (verify via industry data/dealer checks) rather than open-ended exploration without a specific question to answer.
2. Treat scuttlebutt findings as directional evidence, not definitive proof — a single dealer's negative comment doesn't invalidate a thesis; a consistent pattern across multiple independent sources is more meaningful.
3. Prioritize scuttlebutt research for small/micro-cap companies with limited analyst coverage, where this research provides disproportionate informational edge versus widely-covered large-caps.
4. Document the source and date of any scuttlebutt finding used in a thesis — this is inherently less verifiable than filed financial data (Domain 17 hierarchy) and should be flagged as such.

## Worked Example
A company's investor presentation claims "strong festive season demand recovery" for its consumer product. Scuttlebutt cross-check: checking 15-20 retail outlet visibility/dealer sentiment across 2-3 cities shows genuinely improved stock movement and positive dealer commentary about sell-through (not just sell-in to the channel), corroborating the claim. Combined with app store review sentiment trending positive over the same period, this provides reasonable confidence the demand recovery is genuine (sell-through) rather than channel stuffing (sell-in only) — a distinction that financial statements alone (Domain 24's revenue red flags) would only reveal with a lag.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating anecdotal scuttlebutt findings (a handful of dealer conversations) as statistically representative — small samples can be misleading, especially if unrepresentative geographically.
- Using scuttlebutt research to justify a pre-existing bullish/bearish bias (confirmation bias, Domain 11) rather than genuinely testing the thesis.
- Relying on scuttlebutt as a substitute for, rather than a complement to, the mandatory forensic and financial statement screens.
- In litigation/regulatory record checks, distinguishing resolved historical matters from active, material ongoing disputes.

scuttlebutt, channel check, dealer check, ground research, on-ground research, verify company claims, customer feedback research, competitor intelligence.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 24 (revenue red flag corroboration — channel stuffing detection) · Domain 6 (business quality corroboration) · Domain 12 (industry data cross-check) · Domain 8 (management turnover as governance signal).

## Conflict Rule
Financial/forensic evidence (Domain 2, 24) takes priority over scuttlebutt findings when they conflict — scuttlebutt is corroborating/directional evidence, not primary evidence; a positive ground-check cannot override a genuine forensic red flag.

## Universal Rule Applied
Scuttlebutt findings must be explicitly disclosed as qualitative, anecdotal, and lower in the source reliability hierarchy (Domain 17) compared to filed financial/regulatory data — never presented with the same confidence level as verified primary-source facts.

# Domain 43 — Portfolio Management Rules (Swing/Positional Trading)
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready (New Domain)

## Purpose
Domain 10 covers institutional portfolio theory (asset allocation, correlation, rebalancing concepts). This domain provides concrete, actionable rules specifically for active swing (2-10 day) and positional (1 week-2 month) trading in micro/small-cap stocks — the practical rulebook layer Domain 10 doesn't specify.

## Position Sizing Rules

**1. Maximum Single Position Size** — Cap any single swing/positional trade at a defined % of trading capital (commonly 5-10% for higher-conviction setups, 2-5% for speculative/exploratory positions) — never let one position's adverse move impair the overall capital base materially.

**2. Maximum Sector/Theme Exposure** — Cap aggregate exposure to a single sector or thematic cluster (e.g., all defence stocks, all PLI-beneficiary stocks) even if individual position sizes are within limits — correlated positions behave as one large position in a sector-wide drawdown.

**3. Liquidity-Adjusted Sizing** — Position size must respect Domain 29's liquidity guideline (not exceeding ~10-15% of average daily traded value) regardless of conviction level — conviction cannot override structural exit-risk constraints.

**4. Number of Concurrent Positions** — Define a maximum number of concurrent open swing/positional trades based on capacity to actively monitor each — over-diversification in an active trading style dilutes monitoring quality and doesn't reduce risk the way it does in a passive portfolio.

## Entry Rules

**1. Mandatory Pre-Entry Checklist** — Every entry must pass: Domain 24 (Forensic) clean/acceptable, Domain 8 (Governance) no active red-flag cluster, Domain 29 (liquidity) sized appropriately, and a defined stop-loss/target before entry, not after.

**2. Conviction-Tiered Sizing** — Higher position size only for setups with multiple corroborating signals (fundamental screen pass + forensic clean + technical confirmation + optionally smart-money corroboration) — a single-signal setup gets a smaller, exploratory position size.

**3. No Averaging Down on Broken Thesis** — If the original entry thesis (fundamental catalyst, turnaround root-cause fix, technical setup) is invalidated, do not add to the position at a lower price hoping for recovery — this is a Domain 11 (Behavioural Finance) loss-aversion trap, not a valid strategy.

## Exit Rules

**1. Stop-Loss Discipline** — Every position has a pre-defined stop-loss set at entry, sized to the holding-period type (tighter for swing, wider for positional to allow normal volatility) — honor it mechanically, don't renegotiate it emotionally mid-trade.

**2. Partial Profit-Booking Rule** — For positions reaching an initial target, consider booking partial profits (e.g., 30-50% of the position) while letting the remainder run with a trailing stop — balances profit realization with participation in extended moves.

**3. Time-Based Exit** — For swing trades specifically, define a maximum holding period even if neither stop nor target is hit — if a 2-10 day swing setup hasn't played out within its intended window, the original thesis (momentum/volume signal) has likely weakened; re-evaluate rather than holding indefinitely by default.

**4. Thesis-Invalidation Exit** — Exit immediately (independent of price/stop-loss level) if a new forensic or governance red flag emerges (Domain 24, Domain 8) — these override price-based exit rules entirely.

## Risk Budget Rules

**1. Maximum Portfolio Drawdown Trigger** — Define a portfolio-level drawdown threshold (e.g., a cumulative loss %) that triggers a mandatory reduction in position sizing/frequency until performance stabilizes — prevents compounding losses through unchanged risk-taking during a losing streak.

**2. Correlation-Aware Risk Budgeting** — When multiple open positions share a common risk factor (same sector, same macro theme, same smart-money cluster), treat them as a single risk unit for sizing purposes, per Domain 10's correlation principle, but applied at the active-trading position level.

**3. Cash Reserve Discipline** — Maintain a minimum cash/uninvested buffer at all times, sized larger during periods of elevated market-wide valuation or volatility (Domain 13 macro context) — full deployment at all times removes the flexibility to act on new opportunities and increases forced-selling risk during drawdowns.

## Application Framework
1. Every trade decision runs through: Screening (Domain 40) → Forensic/Governance gate (Domain 24, 8) → Position sizing rules (this domain) → Entry rules (this domain) → Pre-defined exit rules (this domain).
2. Review portfolio-level rules (drawdown trigger, correlation exposure, cash reserve) weekly, not just per-trade — active trading requires more frequent portfolio-level discipline checks than a passive long-term portfolio.
3. Log every rule violation (e.g., a stop-loss not honored, a position sized beyond the cap) as a discipline-tracking exercise — repeated violations signal a behavioural bias problem (Domain 11) requiring conscious correction.

## Worked Example
A trader has ₹10 lakh trading capital. Following the rules: max single position = 8% (₹80,000) for high-conviction setups, max 3 positions in the same sector cluster, max 6 concurrent positions total, minimum 15% cash reserve at all times. A new turnaround candidate (Domain 28) passes forensic screen and shows a confirmed technical breakout (Domain 30) — sized at 8% given multiple corroborating signals. A separate volume-story-only candidate (single signal, no fundamental confirmation yet) is sized at just 3% as an exploratory position with a tighter stop-loss given the higher speculative nature.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Position sizing driven by excitement/conviction narrative rather than the pre-defined rules — this is exactly the discipline failure mode Domain 11 (Behavioural Finance) warns against.
- Ignoring correlated sector exposure across multiple "different" positions that actually share the same underlying risk factor.
- Removing or widening a stop-loss after entry because the position is "about to turn around" — this converts a disciplined trade into an undefined-risk bet.
- Zero cash reserve during high-volatility or elevated-valuation market phases, removing flexibility to act on new opportunities or absorb drawdowns.

## AI Trigger Keywords
position sizing rules, stop loss rule, how much to invest per stock, portfolio risk rules, profit booking, exit strategy, risk management rules, trading discipline.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 10 (conceptual portfolio theory foundation) · Domain 29 (liquidity-based sizing constraint) · Domain 11 (behavioural discipline failure modes) · Domain 24/8 (mandatory thesis-invalidation triggers) · Domain 40 (screening as the funnel feeding this rulebook).

## Conflict Rule
Pre-defined stop-loss and thesis-invalidation rules override in-the-moment conviction or emotional attachment to a position — no narrative development justifies abandoning a pre-set risk rule mid-trade.

## Universal Rule Applied
Portfolio rules should be defined and documented before entry, not adjusted retroactively to justify a position already held — any deviation from the stated rules must be explicitly disclosed as a deviation, not presented as the original plan.

## 16. Triple-Filter Quant Momentum Strategy
- **Concept:** Strict systematic multi-factor model merging price momentum with core trailing profitability to eliminate speculative bubble stocks.
- **The 3 Mandatory Filters:**
  1. **Price Filter:** Stock actively trading at its All-Time High (ATH) price.
  2. **Fundamental Filter:** Trailing Twelve Month (TTM) Profit After Tax (PAT) at All-Time High.
  3. **Relative Strength Filter:** Stock actively outperforming both Nifty 500 Index and its Sector Index over a 52-week rolling window.
- **Empirical Win Rate:** Elevates 1-year forward positive return probability from 66% (single-factor price ATH) to 82%.

### Pre-Execution Requirements — Strategy 16
```
□ All-Time High (ATH) price check status (user must provide)
□ TTM PAT historical quarterly data to verify TTM Profit is at ATH
□ 52-week rolling Relative Strength score vs Nifty 500 and Sector Index
```

### Failure Modes & Hard Stop Rules — Strategy 16
```
❗ Stock price at ATH but TTM PAT is declining (speculative narrative bubble)
❗ Stock outperforming benchmark index but underperforming its sector peer index
❗ Discontinuation of PAT growth due to one-off extraordinary accounting items
```

### Worked Numerical Example — Strategy 16
```
Triple-Filter Quant Momentum Execution:
- Filter 1 (Price ATH): Current Stock Price = ₹1,200 (Previous ATH was ₹1,150) → PASS.
- Filter 2 (Profit ATH): TTM PAT = ₹450 Cr (Previous Peak TTM PAT was ₹380 Cr) → PASS.
- Filter 3 (Relative Strength): 52-Week RS vs Nifty 500 = +34% Outperformance; RS vs Sector Index = +12% → PASS.
- Win Rate Upgrade: 3/3 Passed → 82% 1-year forward positive return probability (vs 66% single ATH).
```

## 17. Risk-Based Position Sizing & Pre-Decided Exits
- **Concept:** Systematic portfolio construction where position size is dynamically calculated using a hard pre-decided exit price (e.g. 200-day EMA).
- **Position Sizing Formula:**
  $$\text{Portfolio Allocation \%} = \frac{\text{Max Allowed Risk \% of Total Portfolio}}{\text{Percentage Distance between Entry Price and Exit Price}}$$
  $$\text{Percentage Distance \%} = \frac{\text{Entry Price} - \text{Exit Price}}{\text{Entry Price}} \times 100$$
- **Worked Case Example:**
  - Portfolio Capital: ₹1,00,00,000 | Max Risk per Stock: 1.2% (₹1,20,000).
  - Scenario A: Entry ₹100, 200 EMA Exit ₹80 -> Distance = 20%.
    - Allocation % = $1.2\% / 20\% = 6.0\%$ (Position = ₹6,00,000).
  - Scenario B: Entry ₹100, 200 EMA Exit ₹70 -> Distance = 30%.
    - Allocation % = $1.2\% / 30\% = 4.0\%$ (Position = ₹4,00,000).

### Pre-Execution Requirements — Strategy 17
```
□ Total Portfolio Capital amount (user must provide)
□ Maximum Allowed Risk % per trade (default = 1.2% of portfolio capital)
□ Target Stock Entry Price and 200-day EMA Exit Price
```

### Failure Modes & Hard Stop Rules — Strategy 17
```
❗ Over-allocating beyond the mathematically calculated position size due to high conviction
❗ Moving or waiving the 200-day EMA exit price down when price approaches the exit
❗ Gap-down open below 200 EMA exit causing slippage beyond max allowed 1.2% risk
```

### Worked Numerical Example — Strategy 17
```
Position Sizing Calculation:
- Total Portfolio: ₹1,00,00,000 | Max Risk Cap: 1.2% (₹1,20,000 max loss).
- Scenario A (Tight Stop): Entry Price = ₹100 | 200 EMA Exit = ₹80.
  - Distance % = (100 - 80) / 100 * 100 = 20%.
  - Allocation % = 1.2% / 20% = 6.0% of Portfolio (Position Size = ₹6,00,000 / 6,000 shares).
- Scenario B (Wide Stop): Entry Price = ₹100 | 200 EMA Exit = ₹70.
  - Distance % = (100 - 70) / 100 * 100 = 30%.
  - Allocation % = 1.2% / 30% = 4.0% of Portfolio (Position Size = ₹4,00,000 / 4,000 shares).
```
