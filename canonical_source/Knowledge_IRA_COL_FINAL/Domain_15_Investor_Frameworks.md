# Domain 15 — Investor Frameworks
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Extract publicly observable, repeatable investment principles from successful investors' documented frameworks — without imitating personality, style branding, or making unverified attribution claims.

## Core Principles (Framework-Level, Not Personality-Level)

**1. Business Quality** — Favor businesses with durable demand, understandable economics, and consistent historical performance over speculative or complex models.

**2. Capital Allocation** — Evaluate how management deploys retained earnings: reinvestment, dividends, buybacks, or M&A — and whether each decision has historically created shareholder value.

**3. Cash Flow** — Prioritize actual cash generation over reported accounting profit; a business's ability to convert earnings to free cash flow is a core quality signal.

**4. Scalability** — Assess whether the business can grow revenue without proportionate increases in capital or cost — operating leverage potential.

**5. Competitive Advantage** — Seek businesses with structural moats (see Domain 6) that protect margins and market share from competitive erosion over time.

**6. Valuation Discipline** — Separate business quality assessment from price paid; even excellent businesses can be poor investments at excessive valuations. Maintain a margin of safety.

**7. Risk Management** — Avoid permanent capital loss as the primary objective, ahead of maximizing short-term returns; position sizing and diversification should reflect this priority.

**8. Long-Term Compounding** — Favor sustained, moderate compounding over long holding periods over attempts to time short-term price swings.

**9. Circle of Competence** — Limiting analysis/investment to businesses and sectors genuinely understood, avoiding complexity beyond one's analytical depth.

**10. Margin of Safety (Applied)** — Deliberately buying below conservatively-estimated intrinsic value to buffer against estimation error and unforeseen risk — not just "cheap," but cheap relative to a rigorously defended value estimate.

**11. Contrarian Discipline** — Willingness to act against prevailing market sentiment when evidence supports it, while distinguishing genuine contrarian opportunity from a deteriorating business that merely looks cheap (value trap).

**12. Concentration vs. Diversification Philosophy** — Some frameworks favor concentrated high-conviction positions (fewer, deeply researched bets); others favor broad diversification to reduce single-stock risk — both are internally consistent given differing risk tolerance and research depth, not universally "correct."

## Application Framework
1. Use these as evaluative lenses applied consistently across all research, not as a checklist to selectively justify a pre-formed view.
2. When referencing a specific investor's publicly stated approach, cite only documented, verifiable statements — never fabricate quotes or attribute unverified opinions.
3. Combine multiple lenses (e.g., quality + valuation discipline together) rather than applying any single principle in isolation.
4. Match framework selection to context: concentrated conviction-investing principles don't transfer cleanly to a diversified institutional mandate.

## Worked Example
Applying "circle of competence + margin of safety" together: an analyst understands consumer retail deeply but not semiconductor fabrication economics. A cheap-looking semiconductor stock is passed over not because it's a bad business, but because the analyst cannot rigorously verify its moat or valuation — margin of safety cannot be established without genuine understanding.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Cherry-picking a single principle (e.g., "buy quality") to justify overpaying, ignoring valuation discipline.
- Attributing specific stock recommendations or quotes to named investors without verified sourcing.
- Treating any single framework as universally applicable across all market conditions and sectors.
- Applying concentrated-portfolio principles without the accompanying research depth that justifies them.

## Conflict Rule
When a framework principle conflicts with Domain 9 Risk Management, Risk Management wins — no principle justifies ignoring identified capital-loss risk.
