# Domain 1 — Economics
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Provide the macroeconomic vocabulary and cause-effect frameworks needed to interpret how the broader economy affects companies, sectors, and markets. Economics sets the backdrop against which all company-level analysis must be read.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. GDP (Gross Domestic Product)
- Definition: Total value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period.
- Types: Nominal GDP, Real GDP, GDP at Constant Prices, GVA (Gross Value Added).
- Components: Consumption (C), Investment (I), Government Spending (G), Net Exports (X-M).
- Use in research: GDP growth trend indicates overall demand environment for cyclical sectors (autos, capital goods, real estate).
- Watch for: Divergence between GDP growth and corporate earnings growth (jobless/profitless growth).

### 2. Inflation
- Definition: Sustained rise in general price levels, eroding purchasing power.
- Measures: CPI (Consumer Price Index), WPI (Wholesale Price Index), Core Inflation (ex food & fuel), GDP Deflator.
- Causes: Demand-pull, Cost-push, Built-in (wage-price spiral).
- Impact on companies: Raw material cost pressure, pricing power test, margin compression risk.
- Impact on markets: High inflation typically pressures equity valuations via higher discount rates.

### 3. Interest Rates
- Definition: Cost of borrowing money, set by central bank policy and market forces.
- Key rates (India context): Repo Rate, Reverse Repo, MSF, SDF, 10-Year G-Sec Yield.
- Transmission mechanism: Policy rate → bank lending rates → corporate borrowing cost → capex decisions → earnings.
- Sensitive sectors: Banking/NBFC (spread), Real Estate (EMI-driven demand), Capital Goods (project financing).

### 4. Fiscal Policy
- Definition: Government's use of spending and taxation to influence the economy.
- Tools: Union Budget, tax rates (direct/indirect), subsidies, capex allocation, fiscal deficit targets.
- Key metric: Fiscal Deficit (% of GDP) — indicates government borrowing need, which affects bond yields.
- Research angle: Budget allocations to infrastructure, defence, PLI schemes directly signal sector tailwinds.

### 5. Monetary Policy
- Definition: Central bank actions to control money supply and interest rates.
- Tools: Repo rate changes, CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio), SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio), OMOs (Open Market Operations), forward guidance.
- Stance terminology: Accommodative, Neutral, Withdrawal of Accommodation, Restrictive.
- RBI MPC (Monetary Policy Committee) meets bi-monthly — track for rate decisions and guidance tone.

### 6. Liquidity
- Definition: Availability of money/credit in the financial system.
- Indicators: Systemic liquidity surplus/deficit, banking system credit-deposit ratio, money supply growth (M3).
- Effect: Surplus liquidity supports asset prices (equities, bonds); tight liquidity can trigger de-rating even in fundamentally sound companies.

### 7. Currency
- Definition: Exchange rate of domestic currency vs. major currencies (primarily USD).
- Drivers: Trade balance, capital flows (FII/FDI), interest rate differential, inflation differential.
- Impact: Currency depreciation benefits exporters (IT, Pharma) but hurts import-dependent sectors (Oil & Gas, Capital Goods with imported components).
- Key metric: Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) for competitiveness assessment.

### 8. Bond Markets
- Definition: Market for government and corporate debt instruments.
- Key concepts: Yield curve (normal, inverted, flat), credit spread, duration, yield-to-maturity.
- Relationship with equities: Rising bond yields raise the discount rate used in equity valuation (competing asset class).
- India-specific: G-Sec yields, SDL (State Development Loans), corporate bond spreads over G-Sec.

### 9. Credit Cycle
- Definition: Expansion and contraction phases of credit availability and borrowing in the economy.
- Phases: Expansion (easy credit, rising leverage) → Peak → Contraction (tightening, defaults rise) → Trough.
- Indicators: Bank credit growth, NPA (Non-Performing Asset) trends, corporate leverage ratios.
- Research use: Identify where the credit cycle stands to anticipate banking sector asset quality trends.

### 10. Economic Cycles
- Definition: Recurring phases of expansion and contraction in overall economic activity.
- Phases: Recovery → Expansion → Peak → Slowdown → Contraction/Recession → Trough.
- Leading indicators: PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index), new orders, business confidence surveys.
- Lagging indicators: Unemployment rate, corporate profits.
- Sector rotation: Different sectors outperform at different cycle stages (e.g., cyclicals early, defensives late).

### 11. Global Trade
- Definition: Cross-border flow of goods, services, and capital.
- Key concepts: Trade balance, current account deficit/surplus, tariffs, trade agreements, supply chain realignment (China+1).
- Research angle: Export-oriented sectors depend on global demand; track destination-country growth and trade policy shifts.

### 12. Commodity Cycles
- Definition: Boom-bust patterns in prices of raw materials (oil, metals, agri-commodities).
- Drivers: Global demand-supply balance, geopolitical supply disruption, inventory cycles, dollar strength.
- Impact: Direct input cost for manufacturers; direct revenue driver for commodity producers (metals, mining, oil E&P).
- Key indices to track: Brent Crude, LME metal prices, Baltic Dry Index (shipping/trade proxy).

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Identify which macro variables are most material to the sector/company under study.
2. Map current position within the economic and credit cycle.
3. Assess directional risk (tailwind/headwind) over 12–24 month horizon.
4. Cross-check macro thesis against live data before forming conclusions — never assume stale cycle positioning.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Treating GDP growth as a direct proxy for corporate earnings growth (they frequently diverge).
- Ignoring currency impact on companies with import/export exposure.
- Using outdated interest rate assumptions in valuation models.
- Overweighting a single macro indicator while ignoring conflicting signals.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Macro knowledge explains the environment — it does not replace bottom-up company evidence. Always verify current macro data (RBI releases, MoSPI data, budget documents) rather than relying on memorized figures.

---

## Worked Example
Repo rate rises 50bps (6.00%→6.50%). A leveraged NBFC with ₹10,000cr floating-rate borrowing sees interest cost rise ~₹50cr/year (0.50% × ₹10,000cr) if fully re-priced immediately. If loan book re-prices with a 3-month lag, NIM compresses temporarily until asset yields catch up — check the company's asset-liability re-pricing gap disclosure before concluding margin impact.

# Domain 2 — Financial Statements
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Financial statements are the primary factual record of a company's economic activity. This domain defines how to read, cross-reference, and extract insight from each statement type.

---

## Core Statements

### 1. Income Statement (Profit & Loss Statement)
- Purpose: Shows revenue, costs, and profit over a period (quarter/year).
- Key lines: Revenue from Operations, Other Income, Total Expenses, EBITDA, Depreciation & Amortization, EBIT, Finance Costs, Profit Before Tax (PBT), Tax Expense, Profit After Tax (PAT/Net Profit), EPS.
- Research use: Track revenue growth quality (volume vs. price led), margin trends, and one-off/exceptional items separately from core operations.

### 2. Balance Sheet
- Purpose: Snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time.
- Structure: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity.
- Key sections:
  - Non-Current Assets: Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E), Intangibles, Investments, Goodwill.
  - Current Assets: Inventory, Trade Receivables, Cash & Equivalents.
  - Equity: Share Capital, Reserves & Surplus.
  - Non-Current Liabilities: Long-term Borrowings, Deferred Tax Liabilities.
  - Current Liabilities: Trade Payables, Short-term Borrowings, Provisions.
- Research use: Assess capital structure, working capital intensity, and asset quality.

### 3. Cash Flow Statement
- Purpose: Tracks actual cash movement, distinct from accrual-based profit.
- Three sections:
  - Cash Flow from Operations (CFO): Cash generated from core business.
  - Cash Flow from Investing (CFI): Capex, asset sales, investment purchases/sales.
  - Cash Flow from Financing (CFF): Debt raised/repaid, dividends paid, equity issued/bought back.
- Research use: CFO vs. PAT comparison reveals earnings quality — persistent divergence is a red flag.
- Free Cash Flow (FCF) = CFO – Capex; a critical valuation and financial health input.

### 4. Notes to Accounts
- Purpose: Detailed disclosures explaining line items in the three main statements.
- Contains: Accounting policy details, contingent liabilities, related party transactions, segment-wise reporting, breakup of "other expenses," employee benefit details.
- Research use: Often where the real analytical insight and red flags are found — never skip notes.

### 5. Annual Reports
- Purpose: Comprehensive yearly disclosure combining financials, management commentary, and governance details.
- Key sections: Directors' Report, Management Discussion & Analysis (MD&A), Corporate Governance Report, Auditor's Report, Business Responsibility & Sustainability Report (BRSR).
- Research use: MD&A provides management's own narrative on performance and outlook — cross-check against actual numbers for consistency.

### 6. Quarterly Reports
- Purpose: Interim unaudited or limited-review financial disclosures (Q1-Q4).
- Use: Track trend continuity, seasonality effects, and near-term momentum.
- Caution: Quarterly numbers can be more volatile and less audited than annual figures — treat with proportionate scrutiny.

### 7. Consolidated Statements
- Purpose: Combines financials of parent company with all subsidiaries, associates, and joint ventures.
- Use: Reflects the true economic entity, including minority interests.
- Research use: Preferred for holistic group-level analysis, especially for companies with significant subsidiary operations.

### 8. Standalone Statements
- Purpose: Financials of the parent entity alone, excluding subsidiaries.
- Use: Useful for understanding the core listed entity's performance in isolation, especially for dividend capacity and lender covenants specific to the parent.
- Research use: Compare standalone vs. consolidated to identify subsidiary drag or contribution.

---

## Cross-Statement Analysis Framework
1. Always read all three statements together — never analyze P&L in isolation.
2. Reconcile PAT (P&L) with CFO (Cash Flow) — divergence signals need for investigation.
3. Trace balance sheet changes (e.g., rising receivables) back to underlying business reasons in notes.
4. Compare standalone vs. consolidated to understand subsidiary/JV impact.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Profit growth not backed by cash flow growth over multiple periods.
- Frequent "exceptional items" normalizing otherwise poor core performance.
- Related party transactions embedded in notes without adequate MD&A explanation.
- Restated prior-year figures without clear disclosure of reasons.
- Auditor qualifications or emphasis-of-matter paragraphs.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Never invent or estimate financial figures. Extract all numbers directly from filed statements and disclose clearly if a figure is unavailable or unaudited.

---

## Worked Example
Company reports PAT growth of 20% (₹100cr→₹120cr) but CFO fell from ₹95cr to ₹60cr in the same year. Trace it: Notes show trade receivables jumped ₹45cr — revenue was recognized but cash not collected. Conclusion: reported profit growth is not yet cash-backed; flag as a quality concern pending next-quarter collection trend, not an automatic red flag.

# Domain 3 — Accounting
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Accounting principles determine how economic events are translated into reported numbers. Understanding these principles is essential to interpret financial statements correctly and detect manipulation or aggressive treatment.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Revenue Recognition
- Principle: Revenue recognized when performance obligations are satisfied (Ind AS 115 / IFRS 15).
- Methods: Point-in-time (goods delivered) vs. Over-time (long-term contracts, construction).
- Research red flags: Channel stuffing, bill-and-hold arrangements, aggressive percentage-of-completion assumptions.

### 2. Depreciation
- Definition: Systematic allocation of a tangible asset's cost over its useful life.
- Methods: Straight-Line Method (SLM), Written Down Value (WDV)/Reducing Balance.
- Research use: Compare depreciation rate to industry norms; unusually low depreciation can inflate reported profit.

### 3. Amortization
- Definition: Systematic write-off of intangible assets (patents, software, goodwill where applicable) over useful life.
- Research use: Check amortization policy for acquired intangibles — aggressive useful-life assumptions can understate true cost.

### 4. Deferred Tax
- Definition: Tax impact of timing differences between accounting profit and taxable profit.
- Types: Deferred Tax Asset (DTA), Deferred Tax Liability (DTL).
- Research use: Large DTA recognition based on assumed future profits is a judgment-heavy area — assess reasonableness.

### 5. Inventory Accounting
- Methods: FIFO (First-In-First-Out), Weighted Average Cost; LIFO not permitted under Ind AS.
- Valuation: Lower of cost or net realizable value (NRV).
- Research use: Inventory write-downs signal demand weakness or obsolescence; method changes can distort year-on-year comparability.

### 6. Working Capital
- Components: Inventory + Trade Receivables – Trade Payables (Net Working Capital).
- Cash Conversion Cycle = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding – Days Payables Outstanding.
- Research use: Rising working capital intensity without corresponding revenue growth signals operational stress or aggressive revenue recognition.

### 7. Capitalization
- Definition: Recording an expenditure as an asset (capex) rather than an expense (opex), spreading cost over future periods.
- Research red flag: Capitalizing routine operating expenses (e.g., regular maintenance, marketing) inflates near-term profit and asset base artificially.

### 8. Provisions
- Definition: Liabilities of uncertain timing or amount, recognized when a present obligation exists and outflow is probable.
- Common types: Provision for doubtful debts, warranty provisions, litigation provisions.
- Research use: Sudden reduction in provisioning levels without business justification can be a profit-management signal.

### 9. Capitalization vs. Exceptional Items
- Definition: Items outside normal recurring operations — asset write-offs, restructuring costs, gains/losses on sale of business.
- Research use: Always separate "core" operating performance from exceptional items to judge sustainable earnings power.

### 10. Accounting Policies
- Definition: Specific principles and methods a company applies in preparing its financial statements, disclosed in notes.
- Research use: Compare policies across peers for like-for-like analysis; flag any mid-year or mid-cycle policy changes and understand the stated rationale.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Read the "Significant Accounting Policies" note before analyzing numbers.
2. Compare policy choices (depreciation method, revenue recognition timing) against sector peers.
3. Track any changes in accounting policy year-over-year and quantify their impact on reported profit.
4. Distinguish cash-impacting items from non-cash accounting entries (e.g., DTA/DTL movements).

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Frequent changes in useful life assumptions for depreciation/amortization.
- Capitalization of expenses that peers treat as opex.
- Provisions reversed opportunistically to smooth earnings.
- Revenue recognized significantly ahead of cash collection on a sustained basis.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Accounting judgment areas (provisions, useful life, revenue timing) require extra scrutiny — always cross-check against disclosed policy and flag interpretive uncertainty rather than presenting judgment calls as fact.

---

## Worked Example
A company changes depreciation useful life for plant assets from 10 years to 15 years mid-year. Annual depreciation on a ₹150cr asset base falls from ₹15cr to ₹10cr — a ₹5cr non-cash boost directly to PBT with zero underlying operational improvement. Always flag such policy changes and re-compute like-for-like profit for trend comparison.

# Domain 4 — Financial Ratios
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Ratios convert raw financial statement data into comparable, standardized metrics for assessing performance, health, and value across time periods and peer companies.

---

## Ratio Categories

### 1. Profitability Ratios
- Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue
- EBITDA Margin = EBITDA / Revenue
- Net Profit Margin = PAT / Revenue
- Return on Equity (ROE) = PAT / Average Shareholders' Equity
- Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) = EBIT / Capital Employed
- Use: Measures how efficiently a company converts revenue and capital into profit.

### 2. Liquidity Ratios
- Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
- Quick Ratio (Acid-Test) = (Current Assets – Inventory) / Current Liabilities
- Cash Ratio = Cash & Equivalents / Current Liabilities
- Use: Assess short-term solvency and ability to meet near-term obligations.

### 3. Leverage (Solvency) Ratios
- Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity
- Debt-to-EBITDA = Total Debt / EBITDA
- Interest Coverage Ratio = EBIT / Interest Expense
- Net Debt = Total Debt – Cash & Equivalents
- Use: Measures financial risk and capacity to service debt obligations.

### 4. Efficiency (Activity) Ratios
- Asset Turnover = Revenue / Average Total Assets
- Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory
- Receivables Turnover = Revenue / Average Trade Receivables
- Days Sales Outstanding (DSO), Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO), Days Payables Outstanding (DPO)
- Use: Measures how efficiently assets and working capital are utilized to generate revenue.

### 5. Cash Flow Ratios
- Operating Cash Flow Margin = CFO / Revenue
- Free Cash Flow Yield = FCF / Market Capitalization
- Cash Conversion Ratio = CFO / EBITDA
- Use: Validates whether reported profit is backed by actual cash generation.

### 6. Growth Ratios
- Revenue CAGR, EBITDA CAGR, EPS CAGR (typically 3-year / 5-year / 10-year)
- Use: Measures historical growth trajectory; must be paired with quality assessment (organic vs. inorganic, volume vs. price).

### 7. Valuation Ratios
- Price-to-Earnings (P/E) = Market Price / EPS
- Price-to-Book (P/B) = Market Price / Book Value per Share
- EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA
- Price/Sales (P/S), PEG Ratio = P/E / Earnings Growth Rate
- Dividend Yield = Dividend per Share / Market Price
- Use: Assesses relative cheapness/expensiveness versus peers, history, and growth.

### 8. Return Ratios
- Return on Assets (ROA) = PAT / Average Total Assets
- Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) = NOPAT / Invested Capital
- Use: Measures capital efficiency; ROIC vs. Cost of Capital (WACC) spread indicates value creation or destruction.

### 9. Coverage Ratios
- Interest Coverage, Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) = (Net Operating Income) / (Total Debt Service)
- Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio
- Use: Assesses cushion available to service fixed financial obligations, critical for leveraged companies.

### 10. Quality Ratios
- Accruals Ratio = (PAT – CFO) / Total Assets
- Earnings Quality = CFO / PAT
- Use: Detects potential earnings manipulation; persistently low CFO/PAT ratio is a warning sign.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Never evaluate a ratio in isolation — always compare against (a) company's own historical trend, (b) peer average, (c) industry benchmark.
2. Combine categories: a high ROE driven purely by leverage (not profitability/efficiency) is lower quality than one driven by margins.
3. Use DuPont Analysis to decompose ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage.
4. Pair valuation ratios with growth and quality ratios — cheap on P/E but poor ROIC may be a value trap.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Rising ROE driven mainly by increasing leverage rather than improving margins or efficiency.
- Declining receivables/inventory turnover alongside reported revenue growth.
- Valuation ratios compared without adjusting for accounting differences across peers (e.g., lease accounting).
- Using trailing ratios without considering forward-looking business changes.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Present ratios with the underlying formula and source data used, and explicitly flag when peer or industry benchmark data is unavailable rather than estimating it.

---

## Worked Example
Company A: ROE = 24%, Debt/Equity = 1.8x. Company B: ROE = 22%, Debt/Equity = 0.3x. DuPont breakdown shows Company A's ROE is leverage-driven (Net Margin 6% × Asset Turnover 1.1x × Leverage 3.6x), while Company B's is margin/efficiency-driven (Net Margin 14% × Asset Turnover 1.2x × Leverage 1.3x). Conclusion: Company B's ROE is structurally higher quality despite the lower headline number.

## Conflict Rule
Quality/Cash Flow ratios outrank Growth/Valuation ratios when they conflict — a cheap, fast-growing but low-quality number is a trap, not a signal.

# Domain 5 — Valuation
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Valuation translates business fundamentals into an estimate of intrinsic worth. This domain covers the major methodologies, their assumptions, appropriate use cases, and limitations. Multiple methods should always be triangulated rather than relying on a single approach.

---

## Core Methods

### 1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)
- Principle: Intrinsic value = Present value of all future free cash flows.
- Key inputs: Revenue growth assumptions, margin trajectory, capex/working capital needs, Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC), terminal growth rate.
- Strength: Grounded in fundamental cash generation, not market sentiment.
- Weakness: Highly sensitive to terminal value and discount rate assumptions — small input changes create large valuation swings.
- Best suited for: Companies with predictable, stable cash flows.

### 2. Price-to-Earnings (P/E)
- Formula: Market Price per Share / Earnings per Share.
- Use: Quick relative valuation vs. peers, sector, and historical average.
- Weakness: Distorted by one-off items, accounting differences, and does not work for loss-making companies.

### 3. Price-to-Book (P/B)
- Formula: Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share.
- Use: Especially relevant for asset-heavy businesses (banks, NBFCs, capital-intensive manufacturers).
- Weakness: Book value can be distorted by historical cost accounting, intangibles, and impairments.

### 4. EV/EBITDA (Enterprise Value to EBITDA)
- Formula: (Market Cap + Debt – Cash) / EBITDA.
- Use: Capital-structure-neutral comparison, useful across companies with different leverage levels.
- Strength: Removes distortion from depreciation policy and financing choices.

### 5. PEG Ratio
- Formula: P/E Ratio / Expected Earnings Growth Rate.
- Use: Adjusts P/E for growth — a lower PEG suggests better value relative to growth prospects.
- Weakness: Highly dependent on the reliability of the growth estimate used.

### 6. Dividend Discount Model (DDM)
- Principle: Intrinsic value = Present value of expected future dividends.
- Best suited for: Mature, stable dividend-paying companies (utilities, established banks).
- Weakness: Not applicable to companies that reinvest heavily and pay minimal/no dividends.

### 7. Residual Income Model
- Principle: Value = Book Value + Present value of future residual income (Net Income – Equity Charge).
- Use: Useful when free cash flows are volatile or negative but accounting profits are meaningful (e.g., financial institutions).

### 8. Sum of the Parts (SOTP)
- Principle: Value each business segment/subsidiary separately using the most appropriate method, then aggregate.
- Best suited for: Diversified conglomerates or companies with distinct segments deserving different valuation multiples.

### 9. Asset Value (Net Asset Value / Liquidation Value)
- Principle: Value based on the fair value of net assets, assuming orderly sale or liquidation.
- Best suited for: Asset-heavy companies, real estate, holding companies, distressed situations.

### 10. Replacement Cost
- Principle: Value based on the cost to recreate the company's assets/capacity from scratch.
- Use: Useful floor-value check, especially for capital-intensive industries (cement, steel, power) and as a takeover/M&A reference point.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Select methods appropriate to the business type — do not apply DDM to a non-dividend payer, or DCF to a highly cyclical company without cycle-adjusted assumptions.
2. Use at least two to three methods and triangulate — a wide divergence between methods signals the need for deeper assumption review.
3. Always state key assumptions explicitly (growth rate, discount rate, terminal value) so conclusions are auditable.
4. Compare resulting valuation to current market price and historical valuation bands to assess relative attractiveness.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Terminal growth rate assumptions exceeding long-term nominal GDP growth without justification.
- Using peer multiples without adjusting for differences in growth, margin, and leverage profile.
- Ignoring cyclicality when applying trailing multiples to cyclical businesses.
- Presenting a single-point valuation without a sensitivity range.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Valuation output is an estimate, not a fact. Always disclose the method(s) used, key assumptions, and the resulting sensitivity/range rather than a false-precision single number.

---

## Worked Example
DCF sanity check: FCF₀ = ₹500cr, growth 10% for 5 years then 4% terminal, WACC = 12%. Rough terminal value = FCF₅ × (1+g)/(WACC-g) = FCF₅ × 1.04/0.08 = FCF₅ × 13x. If FCF₅ ≈ ₹805cr, terminal value ≈ ₹10,465cr — this typically dominates 60-70% of total DCF value, which is exactly why terminal growth assumptions deserve the most scrutiny, not the explicit forecast period.

## Method Selection Matrix — Which Valuation to Use When

Derived exclusively from the 10 methods and their stated strengths, weaknesses, and use-case notes already in this domain.

| Method | Best For | NOT Suitable For | Key Risk to Watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DCF** | Stable, predictable free cash flow businesses | Highly cyclical companies (swings distort terminal value) | Terminal value dominates 60–70% of total value — small assumption changes create large swings |
| **P/E** | Quick relative valuation vs. peers, sector, historical average | Loss-making companies; distorted by one-off items | One-off items can inflate or deflate EPS dramatically, creating false cheapness/richness |
| **P/B** | Asset-heavy businesses: banks, NBFCs, capital-intensive manufacturers | Asset-light, IP-driven, or software businesses | Historical cost accounting distorts book value; intangibles often understated |

## Valuation Sensitivity Reference — DCF Terminal Value Sensitivity

Using ONLY the worked example already in this domain (FCF₀ = ₹500 Cr, revenue growth = 10% for 5 years, terminal growth = 4%, WACC = 12%), this table shows how the terminal value multiple changes as WACC and terminal growth rate vary. Formula used: `Terminal Value Multiple = (1 + g) / (WACC – g)` (as stated in the worked example).

**FCF at Year 5 (FCF₅):** At 10% growth over 5 years, FCF₅ ≈ ₹500 Cr × (1.10)⁵ ≈ ₹805 Cr.

**Terminal Value Multiple = FCF₅ × (1+g) / (WACC – g):**

| | **Terminal Growth = 3%** | **Terminal Growth = 4%** | **Terminal Growth = 5%** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WACC = 11%** | 805 × (1.03/0.08) = **~10,365 Cr** | 805 × (1.04/0.07) = **~11,966 Cr** | 805 × (1.05/0.06) = **~14,088 Cr** |
| **WACC = 12%** | 805 × (1.03/0.09) = **~9,212 Cr** | 805 × (1.04/0.08) = **~10,465 Cr** | 805 × (1.05/0.07) = **~12,079 Cr** |
| **WACC = 13%** | 805 × (1.03/0.10) = **~8,292 Cr** | 805 × (1.04/0.09) = **~9,302 Cr** | 805 × (1.05/0.08) = **~10,566 Cr** |

**Key Insight (from the existing worked example):** At the base case (WACC=12%, g=4%), terminal value ≈ ₹10,465 Cr. But moving from WACC=11% to WACC=13% while holding g=4% changes the terminal value from ₹11,966 Cr to ₹9,302 Cr — a swing of nearly ₹2,700 Cr (26%) from a 2-percentage-point discount rate change alone. This is exactly why the worked example notes that terminal value "typically dominates 60-70% of total DCF value" and deserves the most scrutiny.

**Practical Rule:** Always present a minimum 3x3 sensitivity table (WACC × terminal growth) with any DCF output. A single-point DCF valuation without a sensitivity range is a red flag per this domain's existing Universal Rule.

## Valuation Conflict Resolution Protocol — 4-Step Process

Derived from the existing Conflict Rule in this domain (line 102): *"If DCF and relative multiples diverge >30%, trust relative multiples for near-term price action, DCF for long-term fair value — state both, don't average them."*

**Step 1 — Identify the divergence:**
Calculate the percentage gap between the DCF intrinsic value and the current market price implied by relative multiples (P/E × EPS, or EV/EBITDA × peers). If gap < 15%: no conflict, both methods broadly agree. If gap 15–30%: note the difference, state assumptions. If gap > 30%: activate this protocol.

**Step 2 — Determine the horizon:**
Per the existing Conflict Rule — if the decision horizon is near-term (0–12 months), relative multiples have higher weight because they reflect current market pricing and sentiment. If the horizon is long-term (3–7 years), DCF has higher weight because it reflects the fundamental earnings power trajectory.

**Step 3 — Identify the source of divergence:**
The gap is almost always in one of 3 places: (a) WACC / discount rate assumption in DCF vs. market's implied cost of equity; (b) terminal growth rate in DCF vs. implied perpetual growth in the current multiple; (c) near-term earnings estimate differences. Name the specific driver of divergence explicitly — do not leave it as a vague "models disagree."

**Step 4 — Report both, never average:**
Per the existing Conflict Rule: state both valuations and their assumptions side by side. Do NOT mathematically average DCF and relative multiple outputs into a "blended target price" — averaging conceals the divergence rather than resolving it. Reducing confidence is the appropriate response to unresolved divergence, not false precision.

# Domain 6 — Fundamental Analysis
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Fundamental analysis evaluates the underlying quality and durability of a business — beyond the numbers — to judge whether it deserves long-term capital allocation.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Business Quality
- Definition: The inherent strength, predictability, and resilience of the business model.
- Assessment factors: Revenue predictability, customer concentration/diversification, pricing power, asset intensity, cyclicality.
- Research use: A high-quality business converts revenue growth into profit growth consistently across cycles.

### 2. Management
- Assessment factors: Track record of execution, capital allocation decisions, communication transparency, promoter integrity, succession planning.
- Research use: Compare stated guidance in past disclosures against actual delivered results to judge credibility.

### 3. Competitive Position
- Frameworks: Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, threat of new entrants); market share trends.
- Research use: Assess whether competitive position is strengthening, stable, or eroding over time using market share and margin trend data.

### 4. Financial Strength
- Assessment factors: Leverage levels, liquidity position, interest coverage, contingent liabilities.
- Research use: A financially strong company can withstand downturns and fund growth without excessive dilution or distress borrowing.

### 5. Cash Generation
- Assessment factors: Operating cash flow consistency, free cash flow conversion, working capital discipline.
- Research use: Sustainable cash generation is a stronger quality signal than reported accounting profit alone.

### 6. Growth
- Assessment factors: Organic vs. inorganic growth, volume vs. price-led growth, addressable market size, reinvestment runway.
- Research use: Distinguish structural growth (secular demand shift) from cyclical growth (temporary demand spike).

### 7. Capital Allocation
- Assessment factors: History of capex decisions, M&A track record, dividend policy, buyback history, return on incremental capital employed.
- Research use: Evaluate whether management has historically deployed capital at returns exceeding cost of capital.

### 8. Moat (Sustainable Competitive Advantage)
- Types: Brand, network effects, switching costs, cost advantage, regulatory/licensing barriers, intangible assets (patents).
- Research use: Assess moat durability against emerging competitive and technological threats — moats can erode over time.

### 9. Governance
- Assessment factors: Board independence, related party transaction disclosure, auditor quality/tenure, promoter pledging, minority shareholder treatment.
- Research use: Governance issues are often leading indicators of future financial or reputational problems (see Domain 8 for detailed framework).

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Build a business quality scorecard across all nine factors above before forming an investment view.
2. Weight factors based on sector context (e.g., moat and pricing power matter more in consumer; capital allocation matters more in cyclicals).
3. Corroborate qualitative assessment with quantitative evidence from financial statements (Domains 2–4).
4. Revisit assessment periodically — business quality is not static.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Overreliance on narrative/story without financial corroboration.
- Assuming a moat is permanent without monitoring for disruption risk.
- Ignoring capital allocation history when projecting future growth via reinvestment.
- Treating management commentary at face value without cross-checking delivery history.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Fundamental analysis conclusions should always be traceable to specific evidence (financial data, disclosures, verifiable track record) — never presented as an unsupported qualitative judgment.

---

## Worked Example
A branded FMCG company raised prices 8% over two years while volume growth held steady at 5-6% — evidence of genuine pricing power (moat intact). A commodity chemical maker raised prices 15% in the same period but volumes fell 12%, with the gain fully explained by industry-wide input cost pass-through, not company-specific advantage. Same "price growth" headline, opposite quality signal.

# Domain 7 — Technical Analysis

## Purpose
Technical analysis studies price and volume behavior to assess market sentiment and timing. It is a complementary tool to fundamental analysis, not a substitute for business quality assessment.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Trend
- Definition: General direction of price movement over time (uptrend, downtrend, sideways/range-bound).
- Identification: Sequence of higher highs/higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs/lower lows (downtrend).
- Use: Establishes the dominant market bias before applying other tools.

### 2. Support
- Definition: Price level where buying interest has historically been strong enough to halt or reverse a decline.
- Use: Potential entry zones or downside risk reference points.

### 3. Resistance
- Definition: Price level where selling interest has historically capped upward movement.
- Use: Potential exit zones or upside target reference points.

### 4. Volume
- Definition: Number of shares/contracts traded over a period.
- Use: Confirms conviction behind a price move — rising price on rising volume signals stronger trend validity than a move on thin volume.

### 5. Momentum
- Definition: Rate of change of price movement.
- Indicators: RSI (Relative Strength Index), MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence), Rate of Change (ROC).
- Use: Identifies overbought/oversold conditions and potential trend exhaustion.

### 6. Relative Strength
- Definition: Performance of a stock/sector relative to a benchmark index or peer group.
- Use: Identifies leadership and laggard stocks within the market for tactical positioning.

### 7. Moving Averages
- Types: Simple Moving Average (SMA), Exponential Moving Average (EMA).
- Common periods: 20-day, 50-day, 100-day, 200-day.
- Use: Smooths price data to identify trend direction; crossovers (e.g., 50-day crossing above 200-day, "Golden Cross") are widely tracked signals.

### 8. Breakouts
- Definition: Price moving decisively beyond a defined support/resistance level or consolidation range, typically on higher volume.
- Use: Signals potential start of a new directional move; false breakouts are common and require volume/follow-through confirmation.

### 9. Volatility
- Definition: Degree of price fluctuation over a given period.
- Indicators: Average True Range (ATR), Bollinger Bands, India VIX (market-wide volatility gauge).
- Use: Informs position sizing and risk management, not just direction.

### 10. Market Structure
- Definition: The overall pattern of price action — accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown phases (Wyckoff-style framework).
- Use: Provides context for where the current trend sits within a larger cyclical pattern.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Use technical analysis to time entries/exits and manage risk — never to override fundamental business quality conclusions.
2. Combine trend + volume + momentum for higher-conviction signals rather than relying on a single indicator.
3. Always define invalidation levels (stop-loss/support break) before acting on a technical setup.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Chasing breakouts without volume confirmation.
- Using technical signals alone to justify long-term investment in a fundamentally weak business.
- Over-optimizing indicator parameters to fit historical price action (curve-fitting).
- Ignoring broader market/index trend context when analyzing an individual stock.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Technical analysis improves timing, not business quality — this principle governs how it is combined with the rest of the Knowledge Library. It should never be presented as a standalone justification for fundamental investment merit.

---

## Worked Example
Stock breaks above a ₹500 resistance level (tested 3 times over 6 months) on volume 2.5x its 20-day average — a high-conviction breakout signal. Same ₹500 break on volume just 0.8x average, a week later, retraces within 3 days — a false breakout. The distinguishing variable was volume confirmation, not the price level itself.

# Domain 8 — Corporate Governance
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Corporate governance quality determines how reliably a company's disclosed information reflects reality and whether minority shareholder interests are protected. Governance failures are frequently leading indicators of financial deterioration.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Promoters
- Assessment factors: Shareholding trend (increasing/decreasing), pledge levels, track record across market cycles, related entity dealings.
- Research use: Rising promoter pledging is a significant red flag, often signaling personal or group-level financial stress.

### 2. Board
- Assessment factors: Independence ratio (independent directors as % of board), diversity, tenure, attendance record, relevant domain expertise.
- Research use: A board dominated by promoter-affiliated members with limited independent oversight raises governance risk.

### 3. Auditors
- Assessment factors: Auditor tenure, reputation, any recent changes (especially sudden resignations), qualifications/emphasis-of-matter in audit reports.
- Research use: Frequent auditor changes without clear explanation, or resignation mid-term, is a serious red flag warranting deep investigation.

### 4. Related Party Transactions (RPTs)
- Definition: Transactions between the company and entities connected to promoters/management/directors.
- Assessment factors: Materiality relative to revenue, pricing fairness (arm's length), disclosure completeness, audit committee approval process.
- Research use: Large or opaque RPTs (loans to related entities, asset transfers at unclear valuations) are among the most common vehicles for value extraction from minority shareholders.

### 5. Shareholding
- Assessment factors: Promoter holding trend, FII/DII holding trend, public shareholding pattern, pledge disclosures.
- Research use: Sustained institutional selling alongside promoter stake reduction warrants scrutiny of the underlying reasons.

### 6. Capital Allocation
- Assessment factors: History of dividend payout, buybacks, rights issues, preferential allotments, and whether these decisions favored all shareholders equitably.
- Research use: Repeated preferential allotments to promoters at prices below market/fair value can dilute and disadvantage minority holders.

### 7. Disclosure Quality
- Assessment factors: Timeliness and completeness of exchange filings, clarity of segment reporting, consistency of guidance versus delivered results.
- Research use: Vague, delayed, or inconsistent disclosures reduce confidence in reported figures.

### 8. Corporate Actions
- Types: Rights issues, bonus issues, buybacks, stock splits, mergers/demergers, delisting.
- Research use: Assess the stated rationale versus likely actual motivation (e.g., a buyback at low valuation vs. one used to support a declining stock price temporarily).

### 9. Governance Red Flags (Consolidated Checklist)
- Frequent auditor or CFO changes.
- High promoter pledge levels or rising pledge trend.
- Complex group structure with numerous unlisted related entities.
- Large, recurring related party transactions without clear business rationale.
- Consistent gap between standalone and consolidated performance without explanation.
- Delayed or qualified audit reports.
- Whistleblower complaints or regulatory investigations (SEBI, MCA, SFIO).
- Aggressive accounting judgment calls concentrated around reporting periods.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Treat governance assessment as a gating filter — a business with strong fundamentals but weak governance carries elevated risk that may not be fully captured in financial ratios alone.
2. Cross-reference governance red flags against exchange filings, credit rating agency reports, and regulatory action databases.
3. Track trend direction (improving/deteriorating) in governance indicators, not just current snapshot.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Assuming governance quality is static — it must be reassessed each reporting period.
- Dismissing governance concerns because "the stock has performed well" — governance risk often materializes with a lag.
- Relying solely on management's self-reported governance claims without independent verification via filings.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Governance red flags must be explicitly disclosed to the user when identified, even if they do not change the overall investment conclusion — transparency about risk is non-negotiable.

---

## Worked Example
A company's auditor resigns 3 months before year-end citing "pre-occupation of other commitments," followed by a new, smaller/less-known audit firm appointed within weeks, and Q4 results delayed by 6 weeks past the regulatory deadline. Individually each fact is explainable; together they form a governance red-flag cluster warranting a stop on further capital commitment pending clarity — a textbook case of pattern-level, not single-signal, assessment.

# Domain 9 — Risk Management
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Systematic identification and categorization of risks affecting a business or investment, ensuring research conclusions account for downside scenarios, not just upside potential.

---

## Core Risk Categories

### 1. Business Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from the nature of the business model itself — demand uncertainty, product obsolescence, customer concentration.
- Assessment: Evaluate diversification of revenue streams and sensitivity to changes in customer/end-market demand.

### 2. Financial Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from the company's capital structure and financial obligations.
- Assessment: Leverage levels, refinancing risk, currency/interest rate mismatch in debt, covenant headroom.

### 3. Governance Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from management integrity, board oversight quality, and related party dealings (see Domain 8 for detailed framework).
- Assessment: Cross-reference governance red flags checklist.

### 4. Operational Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from internal processes, systems, people, or events (plant shutdowns, supply chain disruption, cyberattacks, key-person dependency).
- Assessment: Evaluate redundancy/backup plans, geographic concentration of operations, dependency on single suppliers or facilities.

### 5. Industry Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from structural or cyclical changes within the sector (technology disruption, regulatory shifts, changing consumer preferences).
- Assessment: Assess the company's positioning relative to industry-wide structural trends over a 3-5 year horizon.

### 6. Regulatory Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from changes in laws, regulations, or government policy affecting the business.
- Assessment: Track pending regulatory changes (tax policy, environmental norms, sector-specific regulation) and their potential financial impact.

### 7. Macro Risk
- Definition: Risk arising from broader economic conditions (interest rates, inflation, currency, global trade) — see Domain 1.
- Assessment: Stress-test company performance under adverse macro scenarios (rate hikes, currency depreciation, commodity spikes).

### 8. Liquidity Risk
- Definition: Two dimensions — (a) company's ability to meet short-term obligations, and (b) market liquidity/tradability of the stock itself.
- Assessment: Company-level — current ratio, cash position, access to credit lines. Market-level — average daily trading volume relative to position size.

### 9. Valuation Risk
- Definition: Risk of overpaying relative to intrinsic value, leading to poor returns even if the business performs adequately.
- Assessment: Compare current valuation to historical bands, peer multiples, and absolute valuation methods (see Domain 5).

### 10. Tail Risk
- Definition: Low-probability, high-impact events (systemic financial crises, geopolitical shocks, fraud discovery, force majeure events).
- Assessment: Cannot be precisely modeled, but should be acknowledged via scenario planning and appropriate position sizing/diversification.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Build a structured risk map across all ten categories for every research subject — do not focus solely on business/financial risk while ignoring governance or regulatory risk.
2. Assign a qualitative severity/likelihood assessment to each identified risk.
3. Explicitly link identified risks to the specific mitigants (or lack thereof) the company has in place.
4. Integrate risk conclusions into the overall research output — risks should influence, not just accompany, conclusions.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Presenting only upside scenarios without proportionate coverage of downside risks.
- Treating diversified risk categories as interchangeable (e.g., addressing only financial risk while ignoring governance risk).
- Underestimating tail risk because it has not materialized historically ("this time is different" bias — see Domain 11).

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Always disclose uncertainty and material risks explicitly, even when they complicate an otherwise favorable narrative — evidence-based risk disclosure takes precedence over presenting a clean investment story.

---

## Worked Example
An exporter with 40% US revenue looks safe on financial risk (low debt) but a 10% rupee appreciation cuts realized revenue ~4% with no operational change — pure currency (macro) risk invisible in the balance sheet. Full risk map must catch this even when financial ratios look clean.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 1 (Macro Risk) · Domain 8 (Governance Risk) · Domain 10 (Risk Budget) · Domain 20 (credit risk corroboration).

## Conflict Rule
When risk categories conflict in severity, weight Governance and Financial Risk above Macro/Industry Risk — the former can cause capital loss independent of business/macro outcome.

# Domain 10 — Portfolio Management
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Portfolio management principles govern how individual research conclusions translate into a constructed, risk-managed portfolio — connecting single-stock analysis to overall portfolio outcomes.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. Asset Allocation
- Definition: Distribution of capital across broad asset classes (equity, debt, cash, gold, alternatives).
- Use: The primary determinant of long-term portfolio risk and return characteristics, more impactful than individual security selection.

### 2. Diversification
- Definition: Spreading investments across securities/sectors/asset classes to reduce unsystematic (company/sector-specific) risk.
- Use: Effective diversification requires genuinely uncorrelated holdings, not just a large number of positions.

### 3. Position Sizing
- Definition: Determining how much capital to allocate to each individual holding.
- Approaches: Equal-weight, conviction-weighted, volatility-adjusted (inverse volatility), Kelly Criterion-based sizing.
- Use: Position sizing should reflect both conviction level and risk (business quality, volatility, liquidity).

### 4. Sector Allocation
- Definition: Distribution of portfolio capital across industry sectors.
- Use: Avoid unintended concentration risk; benchmark sector weights against index composition unless deliberately taking active sector bets.

### 5. Risk Budget
- Definition: The total amount of risk (typically measured via volatility or potential drawdown) a portfolio is willing to bear, allocated across positions.
- Use: Ensures no single position or correlated cluster of positions can cause outsized portfolio damage.

### 6. Cash Allocation
- Definition: Proportion of portfolio held in cash or cash-equivalents.
- Use: Provides downside buffer and dry powder for opportunistic deployment; level should reflect market valuation context and risk appetite.

### 7. Correlation
- Definition: Statistical measure of how asset/security returns move together.
- Use: Combining low or negatively correlated assets reduces overall portfolio volatility more effectively than combining high-correlation assets, even at similar expected returns.

### 8. Expected Return
- Definition: Anticipated return of a security or portfolio based on fundamental/valuation analysis and historical context.
- Use: Should be probability-weighted across scenarios (bull/base/bear) rather than a single-point estimate.

### 9. Rebalancing
- Definition: Periodic adjustment of portfolio holdings back to target allocation.
- Approaches: Calendar-based (quarterly/annually), threshold-based (rebalance when allocation drifts beyond a set %).
- Use: Enforces disciplined "sell high, buy low" behavior and controls drift in risk exposure over time.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Position-level research (Domains 1-9) feeds into portfolio-level decisions — a great business can still be a poor portfolio fit if it creates excessive concentration or correlation risk.
2. Size positions based on both conviction and risk characteristics, not conviction alone.
3. Regularly review sector/asset allocation against both benchmark and absolute risk tolerance.
4. Maintain a rebalancing discipline to avoid emotionally-driven allocation drift.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Excessive concentration in a single stock, sector, or theme, even if each underlying research thesis is sound.
- Confusing diversification (number of holdings) with true risk reduction (correlation-adjusted).
- Ignoring rebalancing discipline, allowing winners to become oversized risk concentrations by drift.
- Holding zero cash buffer irrespective of valuation extremes.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Portfolio recommendations must be evidence-based and risk-aware — never presented as guaranteed outcomes, and always accompanied by explicit acknowledgment of the assumptions and risk tolerance context involved.

---

## Worked Example
Portfolio holds 8 stocks, all in export-oriented IT/Pharma — looks diversified by count but all move together on rupee/US-demand shocks (correlation ~0.8). True diversification needs a low-correlation addition (e.g., domestic-focused banking/utility), not just a 9th stock in the same basket.

# Domain 11 — Behavioural Finance
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Behavioural finance studies the psychological biases that distort investment decision-making. Recognizing these biases — both in market participants and in one's own analysis — is essential for disciplined, evidence-based research.

---

## Core Biases

### 1. Overconfidence
- Definition: Overestimating the accuracy of one's own judgments, forecasts, or knowledge.
- Manifestation: Excessive position concentration, underestimating downside scenarios, dismissing contrary evidence too quickly.

### 2. Anchoring
- Definition: Over-reliance on an initial reference point (e.g., purchase price, past high) when making subsequent decisions.
- Manifestation: Holding a stock because it's "cheap relative to its previous high," ignoring changed fundamentals.

### 3. Confirmation Bias
- Definition: Seeking out and favoring information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while discounting contradictory evidence.
- Manifestation: Selectively citing bullish research while ignoring credible bearish signals on a favored holding.

### 4. Loss Aversion
- Definition: The psychological pain of a loss is felt more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain.
- Manifestation: Holding losing positions too long ("hoping to break even") while selling winners too early.

### 5. Recency Bias
- Definition: Overweighting recent events/performance when forming expectations about the future.
- Manifestation: Extrapolating a recent strong quarter into a permanent growth trend, or a recent crash into permanent decline.

### 6. Availability Bias
- Definition: Overestimating the likelihood of events that are more easily recalled or vivid in memory (e.g., recent news coverage).
- Manifestation: Overweighting a widely publicized risk (e.g., a recent scam) while underweighting statistically more relevant but less publicized risks.

### 7. Herd Behaviour
- Definition: Tendency to follow the actions/decisions of a larger group, especially under uncertainty.
- Manifestation: Buying into a rallying stock/sector primarily because "everyone else is," without independent fundamental validation.

### 8. Narrative Fallacy
- Definition: Preference for coherent, compelling stories over statistically robust evidence.
- Manifestation: Favoring an attractive growth narrative over a rigorous evaluation of underlying unit economics and cash flows.

### 9. Endowment Effect
- Definition: Overvaluing something merely because one already owns it.
- Manifestation: Being reluctant to sell an existing holding even when a fresh, unbiased analysis would not recommend buying it today.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Explicitly stress-test conclusions against each bias before finalizing a research view — ask "would I reach this same conclusion without my existing position/belief?"
2. Actively seek disconfirming evidence, not just supporting evidence, for any investment thesis.
3. Separate narrative appeal from quantitative evidence — a compelling story is not a substitute for verified numbers.
4. Use pre-defined, rule-based frameworks (checklists, valuation bands) to reduce the influence of in-the-moment emotional bias.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Presenting a research conclusion shaped primarily by a compelling narrative rather than verified financial evidence.
- Failing to acknowledge credible bearish or contradictory evidence when it exists.
- Treating past price performance as a predictor of business quality.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Always minimise behavioural bias — this is a standing rule that governs how all research output should be constructed and reviewed, cross-checking every conclusion against the bias checklist above before finalizing.

---

## Worked Example
Investor bought a stock at ₹800, now ₹550. Fundamentals have deteriorated (margin compression, rising debt), but the investor holds on "to recover the loss" — classic loss aversion + anchoring to purchase price rather than reassessing from ₹550 with fresh eyes.

→ Domain 6 (narrative fallacy vs. evidence) · Domain 10 (endowment effect in rebalancing) · Domain 15 (discipline in investor frameworks).

## Conflict Rule
If a research conclusion cannot survive removing the analyst's existing position/belief, treat it as bias-driven, not evidence-driven, and revise.

## Purpose
Maintain a structured understanding of major industries, their key value drivers, competitive dynamics, and sector-specific metrics — enabling context-aware analysis rather than generic evaluation.

## Industry Reference Framework
For each industry, research should establish: (a) key demand drivers, (b) key cost drivers, (c) industry-specific metrics, (d) typical competitive structure, (e) regulatory environment, (f) cyclicality profile.

### 1. Manufacturing
- Demand drivers: Industrial capex cycle, export demand, domestic consumption.
- Key metrics: Capacity utilization, order book, EBITDA margin, asset turnover.

### 2. Capital Goods
- Demand drivers: Infrastructure investment, industrial capex, government spending.
- Key metrics: Order inflow, order book-to-sales ratio, execution timelines.

### 3. Defence
- Demand drivers: Government defence budget, indigenization policy (Make in India), export orders.
- Key metrics: Order book, contract execution timelines, R&D intensity.

### 4. Electric Equipment
- Demand drivers: Power sector capex, industrial automation, renewable energy buildout.
- Key metrics: Order inflow, raw material cost pass-through ability (copper, aluminum, steel).

### 5. Power
- Demand drivers: Electricity demand growth, industrial/commercial consumption, renewable transition.
- Key metrics: Plant load factor (PLF), tariff structure, fuel cost pass-through, regulatory asset base (for regulated utilities).

### 6. Banking
- Demand drivers: Credit growth, deposit mobilization, economic cycle.
- Key metrics: Net Interest Margin (NIM), Gross/Net NPA ratio, Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR), Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR), Credit-Deposit ratio, CASA ratio.

### 7. NBFC (Non-Banking Financial Companies)
- Demand drivers: Credit demand in underserved/niche segments, cost of borrowing.
- Key metrics: AUM growth, NIM, asset quality (GNPA/NNPA), Cost of Funds, leverage (Debt/Equity), liability mix diversity.

### 8. Insurance
- Demand drivers: Financial penetration, risk awareness, regulatory mandates.
- Key metrics: New Business Premium (NBP), Value of New Business (VNB) margin, Persistency ratio, Embedded Value, Combined Ratio (for general insurance).

### 9. Pharma
- Demand drivers: Domestic formulation demand, export markets (US generics, regulated markets), chronic disease prevalence.
- Key metrics: R&D spend as % of sales, ANDA/DMF filings, USFDA facility compliance status, molecule/product mix.

### 10. Healthcare
- Demand drivers: Healthcare infrastructure investment, insurance penetration, medical tourism.
- Key metrics: Occupancy rate, Average Revenue Per Occupied Bed (ARPOB), case mix.

### 11. IT (Information Technology)

### 12. Auto
- Demand drivers: Consumer income levels, financing availability, replacement cycle, EV transition.

### 13. Auto Ancillary
- Demand drivers: OEM production volumes, aftermarket demand, export orders.
- Key metrics: Content per vehicle, client diversification, raw material cost pass-through.

### 14. Chemicals
- Demand drivers: Global specialty/commodity chemical cycles, agrochemical demand, China+1 sourcing shift.

### 15. Metals

### 16. Mining

- Key metrics: Production volumes, reserve life, cost per tonne extracted, regulatory/environmental clearances.

### 17. Real Estate

### 19. Telecom
- Demand drivers: Data consumption growth, subscriber additions, tariff trends.
- Key metrics: Average Revenue Per User (ARPU), subscriber market share, capex intensity (spectrum, network).

### 20. Consumer
- Demand drivers: Discretionary/staple spending, rural vs. urban demand trends, premiumization.
- Key metrics: Volume growth, gross margin, distribution reach, brand market share.

### 21. Retail
- Demand drivers: Consumer spending, footfall trends, e-commerce penetration.
- Key metrics: Same-store sales growth (SSSG), revenue per square foot, inventory turnover.

### 22. Logistics
- Demand drivers: Trade volumes, e-commerce growth, supply chain reconfiguration.
- Key metrics: Fleet utilization, freight rates, warehousing capacity utilization.

### 23. Textiles
- Demand drivers: Export demand, domestic apparel consumption, cotton/synthetic fiber price cycles.
- Key metrics: Capacity utilization, realizations, raw material cost pass-through.

### 24. Agriculture
- Demand drivers: Monsoon patterns, crop prices, government MSP policy, rural income.
- Key metrics: Acreage trends, yield per hectare, input cost inflation (fertilizer, seeds).

### 25. Renewable Energy
- Demand drivers: Government decarbonization targets, PPA (Power Purchase Agreement) tenders, cost of solar/wind generation.
- Key metrics: Capacity addition (MW), plant load factor, tariff realized, project financing cost.

# Domain 14 — Indian Capital Markets
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Regulatory and structural knowledge of how Indian equity markets function — required to interpret corporate actions, filings, and market microstructure correctly.

## Core Concepts

**1. SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)** — Market regulator. Governs listing rules, disclosure norms (LODR), insider trading (PIT Regulations), takeover code (SAST), and investor protection.

**2. NSE / BSE** — Primary exchanges. NSE (Nifty 50) is derivatives/volume leader; BSE (Sensex) is oldest exchange. Track index composition changes for passive flow impact.

**3. Listing Regulations (LODR)** — Mandate disclosure timelines, board composition, RPT approval process, quarterly result timelines. Non-compliance triggers exchange penalties.

**4. Corporate Actions** — Events altering share capital/holding: dividends, splits, bonuses, buybacks, rights issues, mergers/demergers. Each has a record date and ex-date affecting price adjustment.

**5. Rights Issues** — Existing shareholders offered additional shares, typically at a discount, in proportion to holding. Dilutive if not subscribed; assess rationale (deleveraging vs. distress funding).

**6. Bonus Issues** — Free additional shares from reserves capitalization. Value-neutral (proportionate price adjustment) but signals confidence and improves liquidity.

**7. Buybacks** — Company repurchases own shares (tender offer or open market). Reduces share count, can boost EPS/ROE. Assess whether done at fair value and whether it displaces better capital allocation (capex, dividends).

**8. Delisting** — Voluntary (promoter buyout via reverse book-building) or compulsory (regulatory action). Assess offer price fairness for minority shareholders.

**9. Preferential Issues** — Shares issued to a select group (often promoters/investors) at a price per SEBI pricing formula. Watch for value transfer risk if priced favorably to insiders.

**10. QIP (Qualified Institutional Placement)** — Fundraising from institutional investors without a public issue. Faster route to raise equity; check use-of-proceeds and dilution impact.

**11. OFS (Offer for Sale)** — Promoter/large shareholder sells shares via exchange mechanism to meet minimum public shareholding norms or monetize stake.

**12. Block Deals / Bulk Deals** — Large single transactions reported separately. Block: pre-negotiated, above threshold value, executed in separate window. Bulk: >0.5% of company shares in normal trading. Useful for tracking large investor entry/exit.

**13. Circuit Filters** — Daily price band limits (2%/5%/10%/20% or no-band for liquid F&O stocks) preventing extreme single-day moves; triggered circuits signal high volatility/news events needing investigation.

**14. Minimum Public Shareholding (MPS)** — Listed companies must maintain ≥25% public float; non-compliance forces promoter stake dilution via QIP/OFS within prescribed timelines.

**15. Insider Trading Regulations (PIT)** — Restricts trading by "designated persons" during trading window closures (pre-results); track disclosed insider trades (Form 7) as a sentiment signal.

**16. Index Rebalancing** — Periodic Nifty/Sensex constituent changes drive passive fund flows; inclusion typically brings buying pressure, exclusion selling pressure, independent of fundamentals.

## Application Framework
1. Verify corporate action rationale against disclosed filings, not just market rumor.
2. Track record date/ex-date mechanics before analyzing price movements around corporate actions.
3. Cross-check preferential allotment and QIP pricing against SEBI formula compliance.
4. Distinguish regulatory-driven flows (index rebalancing, MPS compliance) from fundamentals-driven price action.

## Worked Example
A company announces a preferential allotment to promoters at ₹450/share when the market price is ₹600 — a 25% discount to market, right at the edge of SEBI's pricing formula (based on 90-day/10-day average). This dilutes existing shareholders' economic interest while promoters gain shares cheaply; verify the formula compliance and audit committee's fairness opinion before accepting the stated rationale.

## Red Flags
- Repeated preferential allotments to promoters at low valuations.
- Buybacks timed to support a falling stock rather than genuine capital return.
- Delisting offers priced below fair value benchmarks.
- Sudden bulk/block deal activity ahead of undisclosed material events (potential front-running signal).

# Domain 15 — Investor Frameworks
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Extract publicly observable, repeatable investment principles from successful investors' documented frameworks — without imitating personality, style branding, or making unverified attribution claims.

## Core Principles (Framework-Level, Not Personality-Level)

**1. Business Quality** — Favor businesses with durable demand, understandable economics, and consistent historical performance over speculative or complex models.

**2. Capital Allocation** — Evaluate how management deploys retained earnings: reinvestment, dividends, buybacks, or M&A — and whether each decision has historically created shareholder value.

**3. Cash Flow** — Prioritize actual cash generation over reported accounting profit; a business's ability to convert earnings to free cash flow is a core quality signal.

**4. Scalability** — Assess whether the business can grow revenue without proportionate increases in capital or cost — operating leverage potential.

**5. Competitive Advantage** — Seek businesses with structural moats (see Domain 6) that protect margins and market share from competitive erosion over time.

**6. Valuation Discipline** — Separate business quality assessment from price paid; even excellent businesses can be poor investments at excessive valuations. Maintain a margin of safety.

**7. Risk Management** — Avoid permanent capital loss as the primary objective, ahead of maximizing short-term returns; position sizing and diversification should reflect this priority.

**8. Long-Term Compounding** — Favor sustained, moderate compounding over long holding periods over attempts to time short-term price swings.

**9. Circle of Competence** — Limiting analysis/investment to businesses and sectors genuinely understood, avoiding complexity beyond one's analytical depth.

**10. Margin of Safety (Applied)** — Deliberately buying below conservatively-estimated intrinsic value to buffer against estimation error and unforeseen risk — not just "cheap," but cheap relative to a rigorously defended value estimate.

**11. Contrarian Discipline** — Willingness to act against prevailing market sentiment when evidence supports it, while distinguishing genuine contrarian opportunity from a deteriorating business that merely looks cheap (value trap).

**12. Concentration vs. Diversification Philosophy** — Some frameworks favor concentrated high-conviction positions (fewer, deeply researched bets); others favor broad diversification to reduce single-stock risk — both are internally consistent given differing risk tolerance and research depth, not universally "correct."

## Application Framework
1. Use these as evaluative lenses applied consistently across all research, not as a checklist to selectively justify a pre-formed view.
2. When referencing a specific investor's publicly stated approach, cite only documented, verifiable statements — never fabricate quotes or attribute unverified opinions.
3. Combine multiple lenses (e.g., quality + valuation discipline together) rather than applying any single principle in isolation.
4. Match framework selection to context: concentrated conviction-investing principles don't transfer cleanly to a diversified institutional mandate.

## Worked Example
Applying "circle of competence + margin of safety" together: an analyst understands consumer retail deeply but not semiconductor fabrication economics. A cheap-looking semiconductor stock is passed over not because it's a bad business, but because the analyst cannot rigorously verify its moat or valuation — margin of safety cannot be established without genuine understanding.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Cherry-picking a single principle (e.g., "buy quality") to justify overpaying, ignoring valuation discipline.
- Attributing specific stock recommendations or quotes to named investors without verified sourcing.
- Treating any single framework as universally applicable across all market conditions and sectors.
- Applying concentrated-portfolio principles without the accompanying research depth that justifies them.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 6 (business quality assessment) · Domain 10 (concentration vs. diversification tension) · Domain 11 (discipline against bias).

## Universal Rule Applied
Do not imitate personalities — extract repeatable investment principles. Any reference to a specific investor's views must be sourced from verifiable public disclosures, never invented.

## Conflict Rule
When a framework principle conflicts with Domain 9 Risk Management, Risk Management wins — no principle justifies ignoring identified capital-loss risk.

# Domain 16 — Special Situations
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Framework for analyzing non-steady-state corporate events where standard valuation/analysis approaches must be adapted to situation-specific dynamics.

## Core Situations

**1. Turnarounds** — Company recovering from financial/operational distress. Assess: root cause resolution (not just symptom management), new management credibility, balance sheet repair progress, sustainability of recovery vs. one-off relief.

**2. Deleveraging** — Active debt reduction via asset sales, equity raises, or improved cash generation. Assess: pace of deleveraging vs. covenant/maturity pressure, quality of assets being monetized, post-deleveraging capital structure sustainability.

**3. Capacity Expansion** — Significant new capital investment to grow production/service capacity. Assess: funding source (debt vs. internal accrual vs. equity), expected utilization ramp-up timeline, demand visibility for the added capacity, return on incremental capital.

**4. High CapEx Phases** — Extended period of elevated capital expenditure relative to historical norms. Assess: near-term free cash flow and leverage impact, execution track record on prior projects, revenue/earnings visibility once capex cycle completes.

**5. Spin-offs** — Separation of a business unit into an independently listed entity. Assess: strategic rationale (focus/unlock value vs. offloading weak segment), standalone financial viability of the spun-off entity, valuation re-rating potential for both parent and spin-off.

**6. Demerger** — Formal split of a company into two or more listed entities, typically along business lines. Assess: fairness of share-swap ratio, resulting capital structure of each entity, whether sum-of-parts value exceeds pre-demerger combined value.

**7. Management Change** — New leadership (CEO/CFO/promoter transition). Assess: incoming management's track record elsewhere, continuity vs. strategic shift signaled, market/employee/client reaction.

**8. Industry Recovery** — Sector emerging from a cyclical downturn. Assess: demand-supply rebalancing evidence, pricing power restoration, which companies are best positioned to capture recovery (balance sheet strength, market share gains during downturn).

**9. Policy Beneficiaries** — Companies positioned to benefit from specific government policy (PLI schemes, tariff protection, subsidy programs). Assess: policy durability/political risk, company's actual execution capability to capture the opportunity, competitive intensity among other beneficiaries.

**10. Cyclicals** — Businesses with earnings highly sensitive to economic/commodity cycles. Assess: current cycle positioning (early/mid/late), balance sheet resilience to survive downturns, valuation relative to normalized (not peak/trough) earnings.

**11. Merger Arbitrage Situations** — Price gap between current trading price and announced deal terms; assess deal completion probability (regulatory approval risk, financing risk, shareholder approval) rather than assuming certainty.

**12. Distressed / Insolvency (IBC) Situations** — Companies under NCLT resolution; assess recovery scenario for different claim classes (secured lenders, unsecured, equity — typically last and often wiped out), and resolution plan credibility.

## Application Framework
1. Identify which special situation category applies before applying standard valuation multiples — peak-cycle or transition-period earnings can be highly misleading.
2. Normalize financials where relevant (e.g., mid-cycle earnings for cyclicals) rather than using latest reported figures at face value.
3. Weight execution risk heavily — special situations carry higher variance in outcomes than steady-state businesses.
4. For event-driven situations (spin-off, merger arb, demerger), build explicit scenario probabilities rather than a single-point outcome.

## Worked Example
A cyclical metals company reports "record profit" at ₹40/share EPS during a commodity price spike. Applying a 15x P/E to peak EPS suggests ₹600 fair value — but normalized mid-cycle EPS is closer to ₹15/share, making a 15x-normalized fair value ₹225. Buying at ₹550 based on peak-earnings P/E would be a classic cyclical valuation trap.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Applying standard trailing P/E to a cyclical at peak or trough earnings.
- Assuming turnaround success based on one good quarter without balance sheet/root-cause verification.
- Underestimating execution risk in capacity expansion or demerger scenarios.
- Assuming merger/deal completion certainty without checking regulatory and financing conditions.

turnaround, spin-off, demerger, deleveraging, capex cycle, industry recovery, distressed, IBC, insolvency, merger arbitrage.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 5 (normalized valuation methods) · Domain 9 (elevated uncertainty = tail risk) · Domain 20 (distressed debt claim seniority) · Domain 23 (M&A-specific special situations).

## Universal Rule Applied
Special situations require explicit disclosure of the elevated uncertainty involved — never present outcome projections with the same confidence as steady-state business analysis.

## Conflict Rule
Normalized (mid-cycle/adjusted) financials override peak/trough reported financials for any valuation conclusion in a special situation.

# Domain 19 — Derivatives & Options
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Derivatives provide hedging, risk transfer, and market sentiment signals relevant to research and portfolio risk management. This domain covers core instruments and their analytical use — not speculative trading strategy.

## Core Concepts

**1. Futures**
- Definition: Standardized contract to buy/sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price on a future date.
- Use in research: Futures premium/discount to spot price (basis) signals market sentiment; rising open interest with rising price suggests fresh bullish positioning.

**2. Options — Basics**
- Call Option: Right (not obligation) to buy underlying at strike price before/on expiry.
- Put Option: Right (not obligation) to sell underlying at strike price before/on expiry.
- Premium: Price paid for the option, comprising intrinsic value + time value.

**3. Option Greeks**
- Delta: Sensitivity of option price to underlying price movement.
- Theta: Time decay — rate at which option value erodes as expiry approaches.
- Vega: Sensitivity to implied volatility changes.
- Gamma: Rate of change of delta itself.
- Use: Understanding Greeks helps interpret institutional hedging behavior and risk positioning, relevant for market structure analysis.

**4. Implied Volatility (IV)**
- Definition: Market's expectation of future volatility, derived from option prices.
- India VIX: Benchmark implied volatility index for Nifty options — elevated levels signal market-wide risk aversion/uncertainty.
- Use: IV spikes around events (earnings, elections, budget) reflect anticipated uncertainty, useful as a sentiment gauge.

**5. Open Interest (OI)**
- Definition: Total number of outstanding derivative contracts not yet settled.
- Use: OI buildup patterns (long buildup, short buildup, long unwinding, short covering) help interpret positioning shifts, often used alongside price action for sentiment reads.

**6. Put-Call Ratio (PCR)**
- Definition: Ratio of put option volume/OI to call option volume/OI.
- Use: Extreme readings (very high or very low) are sometimes used as contrarian sentiment indicators, though reliability varies and should not be used in isolation.

**7. Hedging Applications**
- Portfolio hedging: Using index puts to protect against broad market downside.
- Covered calls: Writing calls against existing holdings to generate income, capping upside.
- Protective puts: Buying puts against holdings to limit downside while retaining upside.

**8. Corporate Use of Derivatives**
- Currency hedging: Companies with import/export exposure hedge forex risk via forwards/options.
- Commodity hedging: Companies hedge raw material cost volatility (e.g., oil, metals) via futures/swaps.
- Research use: Assess hedging policy disclosure — unhedged exposure in volatile input costs or currency is a risk factor (see Domain 9).

## Application Framework
1. Use derivatives data (OI, IV, PCR) as a supplementary sentiment/positioning indicator alongside fundamental and technical analysis — not as a standalone investment signal.
2. When assessing a company, review disclosed hedging policy in notes to accounts/MD&A for currency and commodity exposure management.
3. Avoid recommending specific speculative options strategies; focus on risk-management applications relevant to research and portfolio context.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Companies using derivatives for speculative purposes beyond stated hedging policy (a governance and risk red flag).
- Treating short-term derivatives positioning data as a reliable long-term investment signal.
- Ignoring counterparty risk in over-the-counter (OTC) derivative exposures disclosed in notes.

## Universal Rule Applied
Derivatives content should support risk understanding and hedging context — never presented as speculative trading advice, and always distinguished from core fundamental investment merit.

---

## Worked Example
Ahead of results, a stock shows high call OI buildup at the 5% OTM strike alongside elevated IV — market pricing in an anticipated positive surprise. If results miss, both direction (delta) and volatility (vega) collapse simultaneously, often causing a sharper-than-expected fall — a pattern useful for interpreting post-results volatility, not for predicting direction beforehand.

# Domain 20 — Credit & Debt Markets
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Debt market analysis provides an independent, often earlier signal of financial stress or strength than equity markets, and is essential for evaluating leveraged companies and fixed-income context.

## Core Concepts

**1. Credit Ratings**
- Providers (India): CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings; global: S&P, Moody's, Fitch.
- Scale (long-term, illustrative): AAA (highest safety) down through D (default).
- Notches: Outlook (Positive/Stable/Negative) signals rating trajectory before an actual rating action.
- Research use: Rating downgrades (or negative outlook) are important corroborating signals for financial risk deterioration flagged in equity analysis.

**2. Corporate Bonds / NCDs (Non-Convertible Debentures)**
- Definition: Debt instruments issued by companies to raise funds, not convertible to equity.
- Key terms: Coupon rate, tenure, secured/unsecured, credit rating, covenant structure.
- Research use: Spread over comparable G-Sec yield reflects market-assessed credit risk premium.

**3. Credit Spread**
- Definition: Yield difference between a corporate bond and a risk-free government security of similar tenure.
- Use: Widening spreads signal rising perceived credit risk (company-specific or sector-wide); useful leading indicator alongside equity research.

**4. Bank Loan vs. Bond Financing**
- Bank loans: Typically more flexible, covenant-heavy, relationship-based; may include working capital facilities.
- Bonds: Public/private placement, subject to rating agency scrutiny, wider investor base.
- Research use: Financing mix and diversification of funding sources indicates financial flexibility.

**5. Debt Covenants**
- Types: Financial covenants (leverage ratio caps, DSCR minimums), negative covenants (restrictions on further borrowing/asset sales), affirmative covenants (reporting requirements).
- Research use: Covenant headroom (distance to breach) is a critical but often under-analyzed risk indicator — breach can trigger technical default even if the company is operationally sound.

**6. Default & Restructuring**
- Definitions: Default (failure to meet payment obligation), Restructuring (renegotiation of debt terms — tenure extension, interest reduction, haircut).
- India-specific: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) process, National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) resolution mechanism.
- Research use: Track any restructuring history or IBC proceedings as significant governance/financial red flags.

**7. Yield Curve**
- Definition: Plot of bond yields across different maturities.
- Shapes: Normal (upward-sloping — healthy growth expectations), Inverted (short-term yields exceed long-term — often recession signal), Flat.
- Research use: Yield curve shape informs broader economic cycle positioning (cross-reference Domain 1).

**8. Structured Debt Instruments**
- Types: Securitized debt (pass-through certificates), Perpetual bonds (AT1 bonds for banks — no maturity, coupon skip risk), Convertible debentures.
- Research use: Assess seniority, loss-absorption features, and coupon skip conditions carefully — these instruments carry equity-like risk despite being classified as debt.

## Application Framework
1. Cross-check equity research conclusions on financial risk against credit rating trends and bond market spreads for corroboration.
2. Always review covenant structure and headroom for leveraged companies, not just headline leverage ratios.
3. Track any history of restructuring or near-default events as a standing risk flag, even post-resolution.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Rating downgrade or negative outlook not yet reflected in equity market pricing.
- Covenant breaches or waivers sought from lenders.
- Reliance on short-term borrowing to fund long-term assets (asset-liability mismatch).
- Complex structured instruments (AT1 bonds, securitization) with opaque risk features.

## Universal Rule Applied
Credit market signals should be treated as verifiable, regulator-disclosed data points (rating agency reports, bond market pricing) — never inferred or assumed without current sourcing.

---

A company's equity is still trading near highs, but its NCDs are trading at a yield 250bps wider than 6 months ago, and the rating outlook was revised to "Negative" from "Stable" two months ago. Debt-market signals moved before equity — worth flagging as an early warning even though the stock price hasn't reacted yet.

# Domain 21 — Quantitative & Factor Models
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

## Purpose
Systematic, data-driven frameworks for evaluating and screening securities based on statistically persistent factors — complementing fundamental and technical analysis with a rules-based lens.

## Core Concepts

**1. Value Factor**
- Definition: Preference for statistically cheap securities relative to fundamentals (low P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA).
- Use: Screen for undervaluation candidates; must be combined with quality checks to avoid value traps.

**2. Quality Factor**
- Definition: Preference for companies with strong profitability, low leverage, and earnings stability.
- Common metrics: High ROE/ROIC, low debt-to-equity, low earnings volatility, high accruals quality (see Domain 4).
- Use: Historically associated with more resilient performance through market cycles.

**3. Momentum Factor**
- Definition: Preference for securities with strong recent relative price performance, based on the tendency for trends to persist over medium-term horizons.
- Use: Often combined with quality/value factors to avoid chasing purely speculative momentum ("quality momentum").

**4. Size Factor**
- Definition: Historical tendency for smaller-capitalization companies to generate different risk-return characteristics than large-caps.
- Use: Informs market-cap segment allocation (large/mid/small-cap) within portfolio construction (see Domain 10).

**5. Low Volatility Factor**
- Definition: Preference for securities exhibiting lower historical price volatility.
- Use: Associated with more defensive positioning; relevant for risk-budget-conscious portfolio construction.

**6. Multi-Factor Models**
- Definition: Combining two or more factors (e.g., Quality + Value, or Value + Momentum) to improve robustness versus single-factor exposure.
- Use: Reduces the risk of single-factor drawdowns (e.g., pure value underperforming for extended periods).

**7. Factor Screening Process**
- Steps: Define universe → apply factor filters/rankings → apply sector/liquidity constraints → validate top candidates with fundamental research.
- Research use: Quantitative screening should generate a candidate shortlist, not a final investment decision — always followed by fundamental due diligence (Domains 1-9).

**8. Statistical Concepts**
- Correlation and R-squared: Measuring relationship strength between variables/factors.
- Standard Deviation: Measure of volatility/dispersion of returns.
- Sharpe Ratio: Risk-adjusted return measure = (Return – Risk-free rate) / Standard Deviation.
- Sortino Ratio: Similar to Sharpe but penalizes only downside volatility.
- Maximum Drawdown: Largest peak-to-trough decline, a key risk metric for strategy evaluation.

**9. Backtesting Principles**
- Definition: Testing a factor/strategy against historical data to assess hypothetical performance.
- Cautions: Survivorship bias (excluding delisted/failed companies skews results), look-ahead bias (using data not actually available at the time), overfitting (excessive parameter tuning to historical data).

## Application Framework
1. Use factor screens to narrow a large universe into a research-worthy shortlist efficiently.
2. Always validate quantitative shortlist candidates with fundamental analysis (Domains 2-9) before drawing conclusions — factors identify statistical patterns, not causal business quality.
3. Disclose backtesting limitations (survivorship bias, look-ahead bias) whenever historical factor performance is cited.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Presenting backtested returns without disclosing survivorship bias or transaction cost assumptions.
- Over-optimizing factor parameters to historical data (curve-fitting) without out-of-sample validation.
- Treating a factor screen result as a standalone investment recommendation without fundamental corroboration.

## Universal Rule Applied
Quantitative outputs are statistical starting points, not investment conclusions — always disclose methodology limitations and combine with evidence-based fundamental verification.

---

## Worked Example
A pure low-P/E screen surfaces a stock at 5x earnings — statistically "cheap." Adding a quality filter (ROE > 15%, Debt/Equity < 0.5) removes it because ROE is 6% and leverage is 2.1x — the low P/E reflected genuine business weakness (value trap), not a mispricing. Multi-factor screening caught what single-factor screening missed.

## Cross-Domain Links
→ Domain 4 (ratio inputs to factors) · Domain 5 (value factor ties to valuation) · Domain 6 (fundamental validation of screen output).

## Conflict Rule
Factor screen results are always subordinate to fundamental verification (Domains 1-9) — never output a factor score as a standalone conclusion.

- Research use: FDI policy liberalization/tightening in a sector signals capital flow direction and competitive intensity changes (e.g., defence, insurance, retail).

**9. Sector-Specific Price/Tariff Regulation**
- Examples: Power tariff orders by state regulators, telecom interconnect/spectrum pricing, drug price control (NPPA/DPCO for pharma essential medicines).

**10. Ease of Doing Business & Compliance Changes**
- Definition: Ongoing simplification/reform initiatives (single-window clearances, decriminalization of minor compliance lapses, digital filing mandates).
- Research use: Directionally supportive for corporate efficiency but requires case-by-case verification of actual implementation versus stated intent.

## Application Framework

2. Track pending regulatory/tax changes (draft bills, consultation papers, budget proposals) that could materially alter sector economics.
3. Distinguish between permanent structural policy changes and temporary/scheme-based incentives when assessing earnings sustainability.

- Treating temporary tax incentives or PLI benefits as a permanent part of the earnings base.
- Ignoring pending regulatory orders (tariff resets, price control reviews) that could materially affect regulated businesses.
- Underestimating CCI/regulatory approval risk in M&A-driven investment theses.

Regulatory and tax frameworks change frequently — always verify current rates, thresholds, and policy status against live official sources (government gazettes, regulator websites) rather than relying on potentially outdated figures.

**6. Regulatory Approval Risk** — CCI (Competition Commission of India) clearance for deals crossing asset/turnover thresholds; sector regulator approval (RBI for banking, IRDAI for insurance); cross-border deals may need additional government approval (especially from bordering countries under FDI rules).

**7. Integration Risk** — Cultural fit, key personnel retention, IT/systems integration, customer/vendor contract continuity. Historically the most common reason acquisitions fail to deliver stated value, more often than valuation or strategic logic errors.

**8. Post-Merger Performance Tracking** — Compare actual delivered synergies/margins against original deal-announcement targets over subsequent 2-3 years; management credibility should be judged partly on this track record for future deals.

**9. Hostile Takeovers & Defense Mechanisms** — Open offer requirements under SEBI's Takeover Code (SAST) when acquiring >25% stake; defense tactics (white knight, poison pill equivalents under Indian law are limited) are less common in India versus US markets due to regulatory structure.

**10. Demerger/Divestiture as Reverse M&A** — Selling non-core assets/divisions; assess whether proceeds are redeployed productively (debt reduction, core business reinvestment) versus value leakage.

## Application Framework
1. Separate stated deal rationale from verifiable strategic logic — cross-check against the acquirer's disclosed capital allocation history (Domain 6).
2. Always assess post-deal leverage and dilution impact on per-share value, not just headline deal size.
3. Apply a credibility discount to management-projected synergies based on their historical M&A track record.
4. Track regulatory approval conditions/timelines as a distinct risk factor separate from strategic merit.

## Worked Example
Acquirer (market cap ₹10,000cr, EPS ₹50) buys a target for ₹2,000cr via 100% stock swap, issuing shares worth ₹2,000cr (20% dilution). Target adds ₹150cr to combined PAT. Post-deal EPS = (Old PAT + ₹150cr) / (Old shares × 1.20). If old PAT was ₹500cr on 100cr shares (EPS ₹5... recompute per company context), the deal is EPS-accretive only if incremental earnings-per-new-share exceed the dilution — always run this arithmetic explicitly rather than accepting "earnings accretive" claims at face value.

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Deals priced at large premiums to any of DCF, comparable multiple, or precedent transaction benchmarks without clearly justified strategic rationale.
- Revenue synergy assumptions with no operational specificity (channel, product, geography) behind them.
- Serial acquirers with a history of missing stated post-merger synergy targets.
- Debt-funded deals pushing leverage ratios beyond comfortable coverage thresholds (Domain 4/20).
- Related-party angle where deal counterparty has promoter/management connections (cross-reference Domain 8).
