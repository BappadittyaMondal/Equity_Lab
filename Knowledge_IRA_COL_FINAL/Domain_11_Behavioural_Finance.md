# Domain 11 — Behavioural Finance
Version: 1.0 | Status: Production Ready

---

## Purpose
Behavioural finance studies the psychological biases that distort investment decision-making. Recognizing these biases — both in market participants and in one's own analysis — is essential for disciplined, evidence-based research.

---

## Core Biases

### 1. Overconfidence
- Definition: Overestimating the accuracy of one's own judgments, forecasts, or knowledge.
- Manifestation: Excessive position concentration, underestimating downside scenarios, dismissing contrary evidence too quickly.

### 2. Anchoring
- Definition: Over-reliance on an initial reference point (e.g., purchase price, past high) when making subsequent decisions.
- Manifestation: Holding a stock because it's "cheap relative to its previous high," ignoring changed fundamentals.

### 3. Confirmation Bias
- Definition: Seeking out and favoring information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while discounting contradictory evidence.
- Manifestation: Selectively citing bullish research while ignoring credible bearish signals on a favored holding.

### 4. Loss Aversion
- Definition: The psychological pain of a loss is felt more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain.
- Manifestation: Holding losing positions too long ("hoping to break even") while selling winners too early.

### 5. Recency Bias
- Definition: Overweighting recent events/performance when forming expectations about the future.
- Manifestation: Extrapolating a recent strong quarter into a permanent growth trend, or a recent crash into permanent decline.

### 6. Availability Bias
- Definition: Overestimating the likelihood of events that are more easily recalled or vivid in memory (e.g., recent news coverage).
- Manifestation: Overweighting a widely publicized risk (e.g., a recent scam) while underweighting statistically more relevant but less publicized risks.

### 7. Herd Behaviour
- Definition: Tendency to follow the actions/decisions of a larger group, especially under uncertainty.
- Manifestation: Buying into a rallying stock/sector primarily because "everyone else is," without independent fundamental validation.

### 8. Narrative Fallacy
- Definition: Preference for coherent, compelling stories over statistically robust evidence.
- Manifestation: Favoring an attractive growth narrative over a rigorous evaluation of underlying unit economics and cash flows.

### 9. Endowment Effect
- Definition: Overvaluing something merely because one already owns it.
- Manifestation: Being reluctant to sell an existing holding even when a fresh, unbiased analysis would not recommend buying it today.

---

## Application Framework for Research
1. Explicitly stress-test conclusions against each bias before finalizing a research view — ask "would I reach this same conclusion without my existing position/belief?"
2. Actively seek disconfirming evidence, not just supporting evidence, for any investment thesis.
3. Separate narrative appeal from quantitative evidence — a compelling story is not a substitute for verified numbers.
4. Use pre-defined, rule-based frameworks (checklists, valuation bands) to reduce the influence of in-the-moment emotional bias.

---

## Red Flags / Cautions
- Presenting a research conclusion shaped primarily by a compelling narrative rather than verified financial evidence.
- Failing to acknowledge credible bearish or contradictory evidence when it exists.
- Treating past price performance as a predictor of business quality.

---

## Universal Rule Applied to This Domain
Always minimise behavioural bias — this is a standing rule that governs how all research output should be constructed and reviewed, cross-checking every conclusion against the bias checklist above before finalizing.

---

## Worked Example
Investor bought a stock at ₹800, now ₹550. Fundamentals have deteriorated (margin compression, rising debt), but the investor holds on "to recover the loss" — classic loss aversion + anchoring to purchase price rather than reassessing from ₹550 with fresh eyes.

## Conflict Rule
